\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Following \citet{Long_Lag2025} and \citet{Baumeister2025}, I increase the lag length of
the VAR in \citet{ACEL2005WP} to re-estimate their DSGE. Moving from $p=4$ to $p=12$ lags, I
find that both the VAR and DSGE impulse responses, and the implied structural parameters,
change substantially. The Calvo price parameters that distinguish the observationally
equivalent firm-specific and homogeneous capital versions of the model converge to one
another, from $0.34$ against $0.82$ at $p=4$ to $0.89$ against $0.90$ at $p=12$. The implied
duration of price contracts in the firm-specific model rises from $1.5$ to $9.0$ quarters,
equal to that of the homogeneous capital model, re-opening the micro--macro discrepancy in
price stickiness that firm-specific capital was introduced to resolve.

    \vspace{0.3cm}
    \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Truncation Bias, Long-Lag VARs, DSGE Estimation, IRF Minimum Distance , Nominal Rigidities
    \\
    \noindent\textbf{JEL Codes:} C32, C52, E32
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
% [target: ~2 pp]

An important goal of monetary business cycle research is to explain the empirical evidence
on the impact of economic shocks on macroeconomic variables. Because the systematic component
of policy is endogenous, identifying the causal effect of monetary policy requires isolating
the non-systematic component, the monetary policy shock, even though it is small and
infrequent \citep{Ravenna2007, Aruoba_Drechsel2024_MP_LLM}.

A large class of estimated DSGE models is taken to the data through this channel. Impulse
Response Function Matching Estimation (IRFME) is a limited-information strategy that chooses
structural parameters to minimise the distance between the impulse responses implied by the
model and those estimated from an identified VAR \citep{CEE2005, ACEL2011,
Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}. The strategy is attractive precisely because it is partial:
the researcher takes a stand on the identification of a subset of shocks, and remains
agnostic about the remaining dimensions of the data.

That attractiveness rests entirely on one maintained assumption: that the VAR delivers
\emph{consistent} estimates of the moments being matched. If the reduced-form VAR adequately
captures the data generating process, impulse responses are optimal at all horizons; if the
VAR is misspecified, the specification error propagates to the structural statistics and
compounds at each horizon \citep{Ramey2016, Canova_Fosso2025}
\cite{Ravenna2007} "The bias introduced by truncation can lead to bias in the identification 
of the structural shock."
\citet{ACEL2011} say as much
themselves: ``the quality of our estimation strategy depends on the ability of identified
VARs to generate reliable estimates of the dynamic response of economic variables to
shocks''.\footnote{\citet[][footnote 10, p.~3]{ACEL2011}.}

Since a VAR($\infty$) cannot be estimated on a finite sample, researchers truncate. The
resulting VAR($p$) is misspecified, and increasingly so the smaller is $p$
\citep{Long_Lag2025, Humble_VARs_2026, Ravenna2007}. 
\cite{Ravenna2007} "the problem facing economistis is whether the number of lags $p$ to be 
included is reasonable given the lenght of economics time series over which VARs are
estimated. When estimating a VAR consistent with DSGE business cycle models it is
standard to assume that including few lags is sufficient to provide a reasonable
approximation to the exact infinite order VAR representation!
\citet{Long_Lag2025} show that the
conventional objection to longer lags, that the additional parametrisation makes
confidence intervals prohibitively wide, is incomplete, because reducing misspecification
reduces not only bias but also variance. Their conclusion is addressed explicitly to applied
researchers: ``it is up to the researcher to show that her structural results are robust to
significant increases in the lag length, far beyond the lag lengths typically considered in
current practice''.


\subsection{Contribution and Results}
% [target: ~1 pp]

This paper asks what lag truncation does to \emph{structural} estimates, not merely to
impulse responses. I replicate \citet{ACEL2011} --- henceforth ACEL --- and re-estimate their
DSGE by IRF matching against VARs of increasing lag order.

The contribution is twofold. First, relative to \citet{Long_Lag2025}, I move the question from
the reduced form to the structural parameters. Second, and more specifically, I do so in a
setting their design deliberately excludes. \citet{Long_Lag2025} state that ``all our
experiments are based on two-shock models and two-variable VARs'', and that where a candidate
model does not fit that framework --- Smets and Wouters --- ``we consider the model with only
monetary policy and preference shocks''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~10]{Long_Lag2025}. They also note
that ``invertibility is never a problem; all the models we consider are fundamental''.} Their
data generating processes are square and fundamental by construction, so that truncation bias
is isolated from singularity and invertibility. ACEL's model is not: it confronts three
structural shocks with a ten-variable VAR.
\tbd{Verificare che nessun altro abbia gia' fatto questo esercizio su un modello
stocasticamente singolare. Se confermato, e' il claim centrale.}

My main results are the following. \emph{First}, the impulse responses to a monetary policy
shock retain their sign and broad shape across lag orders, but the humps peak substantially
later as $p$ increases, and the point estimates remain inside the VAR(4) confidence bands.
\emph{Second}, the responses to the two technology shocks --- identified with long-run rather
than short-run restrictions --- do not: several fall outside the baseline confidence set. This
asymmetry is what \citet{Ravenna2007} predicts, since long-run restrictions require the sum of
the entire lag polynomial and truncation error accumulates in exactly that object.
\emph{Third}, the structural estimates move. The Phillips curve slope falls from $0.0402$ to
$0.0129$ and becomes precisely estimated only at $p=12$; the cost of varying capital
utilisation collapses from $1.99$ to $0.004$; the investment adjustment cost roughly doubles.
\emph{Fourth}, and as a consequence, the implied Calvo price parameters of the firm-specific
and homogeneous capital models converge, from $0.34$ against $0.82$ to $0.89$ against $0.90$.
The implied duration of price contracts in the firm-specific model rises from $1.5$ to $9.0$
quarters.

The last result overturns ACEL's central conclusion. Their claim is that only the
firm-specific capital model reconciles macro evidence on inflation inertia with micro evidence
on the frequency of price adjustment. Under a richer lag structure that reconciliation
disappears. The channel is transparent and internal to their own argument: ACEL note that
``to the extent that capital utilization rates can easily be varied, the assumption of
firm-specific capital loses its ability to rationalize low values of
$\xi_p$''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~227]{ACEL2011}.} Long-lag VARs push the estimate of $\sigma_a$
precisely into that region.

\section{The Theoretical Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Inflation inertia and the micro--macro conflict}

\citet{ACEL2011} start from a tension in the New Keynesian literature. To account for inertia
in inflation, standard models require firms to re-optimise prices infrequently --- every six
quarters, and in versions without indexation to past inflation even every two years. This sits
uneasily with the microeconometric evidence, which finds price changes considerably more
frequent. \tbd{Citare Bils and Klenow (2004), Golosov and Lucas (2007), Klenow and Kryvtsov
(2008), che sono i riferimenti di ACEL. Valutare se aggiungere evidenza micro piu' recente.
Da aggiungere a biblio.bib.}

\citet{ACEL2005WP}\footnote{The replicaiton code made available from the authors is NBER working paper from 2005. Hence, I replicate and refere to those results.} 
propose a resolution. In their model inflation is inertial even though
firms re-optimise on average every $1.5$ quarters. Inertia arises not because firms change
prices rarely, but because when they do they change them by a small amount: each firm's
short-run marginal cost is increasing in its own output, which dampens the optimal price
adjustment. The underlying assumption that drives this result is that capital is predetermined within the period
and firm-specific, so that factors of production cannot be instantly and costlessly reallocated across firms.

\subsection{Homogeneous and firm-specific capital}

\cite{ACEL2005WP} conduct the analysis using two versions of the \citet{CEE2005} model: one in which
capital is homogeneous, as in standard formulations, and the other in which it is firm-specific.

Once log-linearised, the only difference between the two sets of equilibrium conditions lies
in the equation relating inflation to marginal cost, the New Keynesian Phillisp Curve,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\hat{\pi}_t = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\beta\Delta\hat{\pi}_{t+1} + \gamma\hat{s}_t \mid \Omega_t\bigr],
    \label{eq:phillips}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{s}_t$ is economy-wide average real marginal cost in units of the final good, and
the slope $\gamma$ maps into the structural parameters as
\begin{equation}
    \gamma = \frac{(1-\xi_p)(1-\beta\xi_p)}{\xi_p}\,\chi .
    \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
The information set $\Omega_t$ contains the current realisation of the technology shocks but
not that of the monetary policy innovation.
The two models are parametrised in terms of $\gamma$ rather than $\xi_p$. 

Given the remaining parameters they are then observationally equivalent with respect to aggregate prices and
quantities, so that no stand need be taken on which version is being estimated. What
distinguishes them is the value of $\xi_p$ given $\gamma$ implies.
\\

The real rigidity parameter $\chi$ measures the strategic complementarity in price
setting of \citet{BallRomer1990}. It takes values in $(0,1]$, and the closer it lies
to zero the stronger the attenuation. That is, a firm that is able to reset its price moves it
by less, for a given movement in economy-wide marginal cost. In the homogeneous
capital model $\chi = 1$ and the attenuation is absent. In the firm-specific model
$\chi$ is a non-linear function of the structural parameters.

In particular $\chi$ depends on some calibrated parameters, $[a,\beta, \delta, \lambda_f]$ which are respectively,
capital component in the Cobb-Douglas production function, time preference parameter, capital depreciation rate, and the average price markup.
And two estimated parameters, $[\sigma_a; S'']$, which are the Capacity utilisation cost and Investment adjustment cost.

The \cite{ACEL2011} logic suggest that, for a fixed value of $\xi_p$, the larger $\sigma_a$, the lower is $\gamma$, in line
with the vast set of literature that suggests that the Phillips curve slope is low.
But other things equal, a lower $\xi_p$ implies a higher $\gamma$. This determine that for a given value of $\gamma$
, $\xi_p$ is a decreasing function of $\sigma_a$.
\\
In this sense, a flat Phillips curve is a problem in the homogenoues capital model.
A low value of $\gamma$ implies that, given that there $\chi = 1$, the Clavo price stickiness parameter $\xi_p$ 
need to be very high, implying irrealistic implied time duration of prices, that is $D = \frac{1}{(1-\xi_p)}$ 
which are in stark conflict with micro evidence.
\\

The flexibility of capital utilisation is governed by $\sigma_a$, and $1/\sigma_a$ is
the elasticity of utilisation with respect to the rental rate of capital. \textcolor{red}{Its role is
easiest to see by following a firm that has just cut its price and must now meet
higher demand. FORSE E' MEGLIO GIRARE IL DISCORSO E PORLO COME SE L'IMPRESA VORREBBE AUMENTARE IL PREZZO} Extra labour can be hired at the going wage, but extra capital services
can only come from running the capital the firm already holds more intensively, and
$\sigma_a$ is what that costs. If $\sigma_a$ is high that margin is expensive, so most
of the additional output has to be produced by adding labour to a quantity of capital
services that barely moves. The firm's own marginal cost then rises sharply with its own output, and
a firm that anticipates this cuts its price by less in the first place. That restraint
is what $\chi$ records, and it is why the firm-specific model needs a high $\sigma_a$:
only then is $\chi$ far enough below one for a low $\gamma$ to be consistent with a
low $\xi_p$.

It is worth being precise here, because $\sigma_a$ is a single parameter, estimated
once, and does not take different values across the two models. What differs is
whether it reaches $\chi$ at all. Under homogeneous capital the firm buys capital
services on a market at the going rental rate, so its own marginal cost does not
depend on its own output and $\chi = 1$ whatever $\sigma_a$ happens to be. Under
firm-specific capital the firm is tied to the capital it already owns, and $\sigma_a$
determines how much that constraint is actually binding.

The limiting case makes the point. If $\sigma_a$ is close to zero, utilisation is
almost free to vary, so a firm tied to its own capital can still draw from it whatever
volume of capital services it needs, at negligible cost. Owning the capital rather
than renting it then makes no practical difference: the firm's own marginal cost is as
flat as it would be with a market for capital services, $\chi$ returns to one, and the
firm-specific model collapses onto the homogeneous one. Firm specificity is an
assumption about ownership, but its economic content comes entirely from the cost of
varying utilisation, and without that cost the constraint exists in name only.
\\

This is where the mechanism is fragile, and \cite{ACEL2011} say themselves so: "to the extent
that capital utilization rates can easily be varied, the assumption of firm-specific
capital loses its ability to rationalize low values of
$\xi_p$".\footnote{\citet[][p.~227]{ACEL2011}.} The reconciliation of micro and macro
evidence rests, in the end, lies on a single estimated parameter.

\subsection{DSGE estimation via IRF matching}
Theoretical models are usefull to economists and policymakers only if they are able to reproduce important
feauters of the observed data. Which is a property that critically depends on the values that model's 
parameters have attached, and also on how the researcher decide to estimate them \cite{Theodoridis2011}.

Folowing \cite{RotembergWoodford1997, CEE2005, Iacoviello2005, BoivinGiannoni2006}, \cite{ACEL2005WP} 
use a limited information approach that minimizes the distance between sample and DSGE model generated impulse responses,
this is called Impulse Response Function Matching Estimator (IRFME) \cite{Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}.
This procedure select and estimate the model's structural parameters that produce the DSGE IRFs, which
minimises the distance with IRFs created from the SVAR. 

Using an IRFME implies that the researcher is focusing to match on some limited aspects of the DSGE \cite{Giacomini2013}; 
it is choosing an identification strategy for the reduced form VAR in a way that is consistent with the key determinants of the DSGE.
The VAR and theoretical IRFs are identified by restrictions implied by the DSGE. Which means that the DSGE 
admits a structural VAR representation, in this way, sample moments of the VAR are informative for the 
theoretical model's structural parameters \cite{Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}.

\cite{Theodoridis2011} points on \textcolor{red}{Canova (1994) and (2005)} tought that; many DSGE economistis prefer to use 
partial information estimators rather than full information ones, like maximum likelihood, because they 
prefere estimates that minimise the economic distance rather than statistical fit criteria.
Because structual IRF matching estimates deliver selected DSGE moments-statistic as close as possible to 
those observed in the data.
The economist can assess how well the estimated model replicates these targets allowing the researcher 
to identify possible modelling weaknesses and to infer usefull economic conclusions.
\\

The authors use a four lag medium scale VAR with 10 quarterly series and 3 shocks: Monetary Policy Shock identified with 
short run Cholesky restriction, and Embodied and Neutral Technology shocks with long run identification à la 
\cite{Shapiro_Watson88}. As well summarised by \cite{Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}, the theoretical proposed by
\cite{ACEL2005WP}  impose that the tech shocks are the only one allowed to affect productivity in the long run. 
In particular, only Capital Embodied shock can affects the price of investment goods.
Finally, Monetary Policy shock affects quantities and prices only within a quarter. 
The VAR is identified such its IRFs respects the DSGE restrictions and estimate the structural parameters.

\subsection{SVAR and IRF minimum distance}
The SVAR can be recovered by imposing structure on the reduced form,
\begin{equation}
    B_0^{-1}B_0Y_t = B_0^{-1}B_1Y_{t-1} + \dots + B_0^{-1}B_pY_{t-p} + B_0^{-1}w_t,
\end{equation}
which matches the RF-VAR term by term once $A_i = B_0^{-1}B_i$ for each lag $i$ and
\begin{equation}
    u_t = B_0^{-1}w_t .
    \label{eq:u-w-map}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:u-w-map} is the bridge between the two representations: the
reduced-form residuals are a linear combination of the structural shocks. Taking
variances on both sides,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_u = B_0^{-1}\Sigma_w\bigl(B_0^{-1}\bigr)' .
    \label{eq:sigma-map}
\end{equation}
$\Sigma_w$ is the variance-covariance matrix of the structural shocks $w_t$. These
are assumed mutually orthogonal and normalised to unit variance, so $\Sigma_w = I_K$
by construction. This is a normalisation, not an assumption about the economy: what
is estimated is not the scale of each shock, but how a given shock propagates through
the system, which is entirely encoded in $B_0^{-1}$.

$Y_t$ is the ten-dimensional vector of quarterly series used by \citet{ACEL2005WP,ACEL2011},
\begin{equation}
    Y_t =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \Delta\ln(\text{relative price of investment}_t) \\
        \Delta\ln(\text{GDP}_t/\text{Hours}_t) \\
        \Delta\ln(\text{GDP deflator}_t) \\
        \text{Capacity utilisation}_t \\
        \ln(\text{Hours}_t) \\
        \ln(\text{GDP}_t/\text{Hours}_t) - \ln(W_t/P_t) \\
        \ln(C_t/\text{GDP}_t) \\
        \ln(I_t/\text{GDP}_t) \\
        \text{Federal funds rate}_t \\
        \ln(\text{GDP deflator}_t) + \ln(\text{GDP}_t) - \ln(\text{MZM}_t)
    \end{pmatrix} .
    \label{eq:Yt}
\end{equation}
All the variables are quarterly series from the 1959Q2 - 2001Q4 period, more details on data 
are described in Appendix \ref{app:data}.

Identification combines a short-run and a long-run restriction. The monetary policy
shock is identified recursively, following \citet{CEE2005}: the interest rate reacts
to lagged variables and to a subset of fast-moving contemporaneous variables, but the
shock is assumed orthogonal to that information set, which amounts to zero
restrictions on $B_0$. The two technology shocks are identified through long-run
restrictions in the tradition of \citet{Shapiro_Watson88}: both
neutral and capital-embodied technology shocks are the only shocks allowed to affect
the long-run level of labour productivity, and the capital-embodied shock alone is
allowed to affect the long-run relative price of investment. The full derivation
is in Appendix \ref{app:identification}.

These restrictions identify three shocks out of ten. \citet{ACEL2005WP} note that the
remaining six are left unlabelled. This SVAR identifies selected aspects that are consistent
with the DSGE's shocks. IRFME matches the IRFs of the three identified shocks, 
not on those not identified.

\subsection{Estimating the Parameters of the Equilibrium Model}
Using \cite{ACEL2005WP,ACEL2011} notation, let $\zeta$ denote the strucutal model parameters that are aimed to be estimated, and $\Psi(\zeta)$ be the mapping 
from the structural parameters $\zeta$ to the model IRFs. The SVAR IRFs estimates are denoted as $\hat{\Psi}$.

The IRFME then can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\zeta} = \underset{\zeta}{\arg\min} \, (\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\zeta))' V^{-1} (\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\zeta))
\end{equation}
The IRF matching is done horizon by horizon: the estimator compares the VAR
point estimate of each impulse response at each of the first 20 horizons, excluding
those set to zero by identification, against the model-implied counterpart. 
Here, V is a diagonal matrix composed by the sample variances along the diagonal $\hat{\Psi}$.
Those variances, compose the condifence intervals around the point estimates of the SVAR IRFs.
With this choice of V, the estimated model's structural parameters $\hat{\zeta}$ are the value 
of ${\zeta}$ that ensures that the $\hat{\Psi}$ DSGE's IRFs lies as much as possible inside the VAR IRFs' confidence intervals.
Standard errors of the DSGE's structural parameters $\hat{\zeta}$ are computed using the Deltha method, 
more detail in the Appendix \ref{app:delta_method}

The minimum distance estimator relies on the ability of the VAR to estimate the IRFs.
\cite{Ramey2016} p. 83 point the attention on potential issues in general VAR practice:
 \textit{"if the VAR is misspecified, the specification errors will be computed at each horizon"}.
This is clear to the author which claim \cite{ACEL2011} \textit{"The quality of our strategy (IRF matching) 
depends on the ability of identified VARs to generate reliable estimates of the
dynamic response of economic variables to shocks"} p. 3, fn. 10.

\section{Truncation Bias and the Case for Long-Lag VARs}
\label{sec:truncation}

Many DSGE's log linearised solution can be mapped in State Space representation, and then under some assumption
can be re-written as VAR($\infty$) \cite{FVRRS2005}. Standard lag choice by SVAR practitioner is to 
use forecasting information criteria (IC) such as AIC BIC, but the most common choice is 
use conventions, that is, 4 lags for quarterly data and 12 for monthly data \cite{Long_Lag2025}.
If a DSGE is estimated using an IRF matching tecnique, using a short lag VAR it might be problematic
as the DSGE is estimated by a biased by truncation VAR.
VARs are typically small systems with few lags often interpreted as models of the entire economy, 
IRFs are highly sensitive to variable and lag choice \cite{Humble_VARs_2026,Long_Lag2025}.

Truncation Bias it might be a potential problem for structual inference.

%VARs are subject to at least three sources of bias: small-sample bias, identification bias
%\citep{Forni_Gambetti_Sala2016}, and truncation bias \citep{Long_Lag2025, Ravenna2007,
%ChariKehoeMcGrattan2008, Humble_VARs_2026}. This paper is concerned with the latter, and with
%the way it contaminates the second.

\subsection{Illustration of the problem: Truncation Bias}

Following \citet{Long_Lag2025}, consider a data generating process in which $y_t$ depends on
two exogenous variables,
\begin{equation}
    y_t = x_{1t}\beta_1 + x_{2t}\beta_2 + \epsilon_t .
\end{equation}
Estimating the relationship while omitting $x_{2t}$,
\begin{equation}
    y_t = x_{1t} b_1 + e_t ,
\end{equation}
delivers a biased point estimate unless the regressors are orthogonal,
$\mathbb{E}(b_1)\neq\beta_1$, and an upward-biased residual variance, $s^2 > \sigma^2$. Both
distortions matter here, and the second is the one usually forgotten.

The same logic carries over to a VAR. Let
\begin{equation}
    Y_t = B_1 Y_{t-1} + \dots + B_p Y_{t-p} + u_t,
    \qquad \mathbb{E}[u_t u_t'] = \Sigma_u ,
    \label{eq:var}
\end{equation}
with $B(L) = B_1 L + \dots + B_p L^p$ and $p \ll p^{*}$, where $p^{*}$ denotes the true lag
order. Truncation biases both the autoregressive coefficients $B(L)$ and the reduced-form
covariance matrix $\Sigma_u$. Since impulse responses are a function of both,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IRF} = f\bigl(B(L), \Sigma_u\bigr),
\end{equation}
they inherit the bias.

\section{Identification strategy}
\label{app:identification}
Equation \eqref{eq:sigma-map} also shows why identification requires restrictions.
$\Sigma_u$ has $K(K+1)/2$ distinct elements, but $B_0^{-1}$ has $K^2$ free entries, so
$K(K-1)/2$ restrictions on $B_0$ are needed before $B_0^{-1}$ can be recovered from
$\Sigma_u$. Being numerically sufficient is not enough: the restrictions have to be
economically motivated, and it is exactly this requirement that the DSGE is used to
satisfy.
