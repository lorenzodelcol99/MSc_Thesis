Yes. Following \citet{Long_Lag2025} and \citet{Baumeister2025}, I increase the lag length of

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
% [target: ~2 pp]

An important goal of monetary business cycle research is to explain the empirical evidence
on the impact of economic shocks on macroeconomic variables. Because the systematic component
of policy is endogenous, identifying the causal effect of monetary policy requires isolating
the non-systematic component, the monetary policy shock, even though it is small and
infrequent \citep{Ravenna2007, Aruoba_Drechsel2024_MP_LLM}.

A large class of estimated DSGE models is taken to the data through this channel. Impulse
Response Function Matching Estimation (IRFME) is a limited-information strategy that chooses
structural parameters to minimise the distance between the impulse responses implied by the
model and those estimated from an identified VAR \citep{CEE2005, ACEL2011,
Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}. The strategy is attractive precisely because it is partial:
the researcher takes a stand on the identification of a subset of shocks, and remains
agnostic about the remaining dimensions of the data.

That attractiveness rests entirely on one maintained assumption: that the VAR delivers
\emph{consistent} estimates of the moments being matched. If the reduced-form VAR adequately
captures the data generating process, impulse responses are optimal at all horizons; if the
VAR is misspecified, the specification error propagates to the structural statistics and
compounds at each horizon \citep{Ramey2016, Canova_Fosso2025}
\cite{Ravenna2007} "The bias introduced by truncation can lead to bias in the identification 
of the structural shock."
\citet{ACEL2011} say as much
themselves: ``the quality of our estimation strategy depends on the ability of identified
VARs to generate reliable estimates of the dynamic response of economic variables to
shocks''.\footnote{\citet[][footnote 10, p.~3]{ACEL2011}.}

Since a VAR($\infty$) cannot be estimated on a finite sample, researchers truncate. The
resulting VAR($p$) is misspecified, and increasingly so the smaller is $p$
\citep{Long_Lag2025, Humble_VARs_2026, Ravenna2007}. 
\cite{Ravenna2007} "the problem facing economistis is whether the number of lags $p$ to be 
included is reasonable given the lenght of economics time series over which VARs are
estimated. When estimating a VAR consistent with DSGE business cycle models it is
standard to assume that including few lags is sufficient to provide a reasonable
approximation to the exact infinite order VAR representation!
\citet{Long_Lag2025} show that the
conventional objection to longer lags, that the additional parametrisation makes
confidence intervals prohibitively wide, is incomplete, because reducing misspecification
reduces not only bias but also variance. Their conclusion is addressed explicitly to applied
researchers: ``it is up to the researcher to show that her structural results are robust to
significant increases in the lag length, far beyond the lag lengths typically considered in
current practice''.

\subsection{Contribution and Results}
% [target: ~1 pp]

This paper asks what lag truncation does to \emph{structural} estimates, not merely to
impulse responses. I replicate \citet{ACEL2011} --- henceforth ACEL --- and re-estimate their
DSGE by IRF matching against VARs of increasing lag order.

The contribution is twofold. First, relative to \citet{Long_Lag2025}, I move the question from
the reduced form to the structural parameters. Second, and more specifically, I do so in a
setting their design deliberately excludes. \citet{Long_Lag2025} state that ``all our
experiments are based on two-shock models and two-variable VARs'', and that where a candidate
model does not fit that framework --- Smets and Wouters --- ``we consider the model with only
monetary policy and preference shocks''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~10]{Long_Lag2025}. They also note
that ``invertibility is never a problem; all the models we consider are fundamental''.} Their
data generating processes are square and fundamental by construction, so that truncation bias
is isolated from singularity and invertibility. ACEL's model is not: it confronts three
structural shocks with a ten-variable VAR.
\tbd{Verificare che nessun altro abbia gia' fatto questo esercizio su un modello
stocasticamente singolare. Se confermato, e' il claim centrale.}

My main results are the following. \emph{First}, the impulse responses to a monetary policy
shock retain their sign and broad shape across lag orders, but the humps peak substantially
later as $p$ increases, and the point estimates remain inside the VAR(4) confidence bands.
\emph{Second}, the responses to the two technology shocks --- identified with long-run rather
than short-run restrictions --- do not: several fall outside the baseline confidence set. This
asymmetry is what \citet{Ravenna2007} predicts, since long-run restrictions require the sum of
the entire lag polynomial and truncation error accumulates in exactly that object.
\emph{Third}, the structural estimates move. The Phillips curve slope falls from $0.0402$ to
$0.0129$ and becomes precisely estimated only at $p=12$; the cost of varying capital
utilisation collapses from $1.99$ to $0.004$; the investment adjustment cost roughly doubles.
\emph{Fourth}, and as a consequence, the implied Calvo price parameters of the firm-specific
and homogeneous capital models converge, from $0.34$ against $0.82$ to $0.89$ against $0.90$.
The implied duration of price contracts in the firm-specific model rises from $1.5$ to $9.0$
quarters.

The last result overturns ACEL's central conclusion. Their claim is that only the
firm-specific capital model reconciles macro evidence on inflation inertia with micro evidence
on the frequency of price adjustment. Under a richer lag structure that reconciliation
disappears. The channel is transparent and internal to their own argument: ACEL note that
``to the extent that capital utilization rates can easily be varied, the assumption of
firm-specific capital loses its ability to rationalize low values of
$\xi_p$''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~227]{ACEL2011}.} Long-lag VARs push the estimate of $\sigma_a$
precisely into that region.

\section{Truncation Bias and the Case for Long-Lag VARs}
\label{sec:truncation}

VARs are subject to at least three sources of bias: small-sample bias, identification bias
\citep{Forni_Gambetti_Sala2016}, and truncation bias \citep{Long_Lag2025, Ravenna2007,
ChariKehoeMcGrattan2008, Humble_VARs_2026}. This paper is concerned with the latter, and with
the way it contaminates the second.

\subsection{A simple illustration}

Following \citet{Long_Lag2025}, consider a data generating process in which $y_t$ depends on
two exogenous variables,
\begin{equation}
    y_t = x_{1t}\beta_1 + x_{2t}\beta_2 + \epsilon_t .
\end{equation}
Estimating the relationship while omitting $x_{2t}$,
\begin{equation}
    y_t = x_{1t} b_1 + e_t ,
\end{equation}
delivers a biased point estimate unless the regressors are orthogonal,
$\mathbb{E}(b_1)\neq\beta_1$, and an upward-biased residual variance, $s^2 > \sigma^2$. Both
distortions matter here, and the second is the one usually forgotten.

The same logic carries over to a VAR. Let
\begin{equation}
    Y_t = B_1 Y_{t-1} + \dots + B_p Y_{t-p} + u_t,
    \qquad \mathbb{E}[u_t u_t'] = \Sigma_u ,
    \label{eq:var}
\end{equation}
with $B(L) = B_1 L + \dots + B_p L^p$ and $p \ll p^{*}$, where $p^{*}$ denotes the true lag
order. Truncation biases both the autoregressive coefficients $B(L)$ and the reduced-form
covariance matrix $\Sigma_u$. Since impulse responses are a function of both,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IRF} = f\bigl(B(L), \Sigma_u\bigr),
\end{equation}
they inherit the bias.

\subsection{Two channels: pure truncation and identification bias}

\citet{Ravenna2007} shows that the distortion operates through two separate channels. The
first is \emph{pure truncation bias}: a VAR($p$) restricts all coefficients beyond lag $p$ to
zero and therefore does not describe the true dynamics of the model. The second is
\emph{identification bias}: when the autoregressive coefficients enter the matrix mapping
reduced-form innovations into structural shocks, truncation corrupts that mapping. Crucially,
the identification bias arises \emph{even when the identifying assumptions are correct}, and
both results are established in population, so that neither is an artefact of small samples.

Two implications matter for what follows. First, \citet{Ravenna2007} finds the identification
channel to be the dominant one. Second, the two channels do not affect all identification
schemes equally. Schemes based on long-run restrictions require the sum
$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Psi_i$ of the entire lag polynomial, and that sum can be estimated very
poorly even when the individual coefficients are estimated
tightly.\footnote{\citet{Ravenna2007}; see also the discussion in \citet{CEV2006}.
\tbd{Aggiungere a biblio.bib: Sims (1972), Faust and Leeper (1997), Erceg, Guerrieri and Gust
(2005). Gli ultimi due sono gia' nella cartella ``Letteratura x Tesi''.}}
\citet{Ravenna2007} documents parametrisations in which the VAR($p$) approximates the
infinite-order representation well coefficient by coefficient and nonetheless tracks the
long-run identified response poorly. This asymmetry between short-run and long-run
identification generates a testable prediction, which I take to the data in Section
\ref{sec:results_tech}.

\subsection{Why longer lags help}
\label{sec:whylonger}

\citet{Long_Lag2025} explore the obvious remedy, increasing $p$ substantially, and find
evidence against the conventional wisdom that uncertainty becomes prohibitive. The standard
intuition is only part of the story: raising the lag order reduces misspecification, and
reducing misspecification lowers not only bias but also the variance of the residuals. This
variance reduction works against the imprecision induced by the additional parameters, and in
their simulations on realistically sized samples it frequently dominates. The result is
reduced truncation bias, more precise inference, lower mean squared error and better coverage.

A corollary concerns model selection. Information criteria are built to optimise
one-step-ahead forecasting, an out-of-sample objective that rewards parsimony; structural
inference is a different problem. \citet{Long_Lag2025} put it directly: ``information criteria
focused purely on forecasting are poor guides for accurate structural
inference''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~6]{Long_Lag2025}.} This matters here because ACEL report
that the Akaike, Hannan--Quinn and Schwartz criteria support $q = 2, 2, 1$ respectively ---
standard criteria point towards \emph{fewer} lags than even their own baseline of four.

\tbd{Affrontare qui, di petto, il punto debole dell'esercizio, prima che lo faccia il lettore:
RMSE in-sample e generalized variance migliorano meccanicamente all'aumentare di $p$, quindi
non costituiscono prova di specificazione migliore. La posizione da tenere e' che non
sostengo che $p=12$ sia il valore ``vero'': sostengo che le conclusioni dipendono da $p$ e che
questa sensibilita' vada riportata come prassi. Questo elimina il problema di model selection
invece di provare a risolverlo.}

\subsection{Inference beyond the truncation horizon}
\label{sec:hgtp}

A third result of \citet{Long_Lag2025} bears directly on how impulse responses should be read.
They argue that a VAR($p$) should not be used for structural inference at horizons $h > p$,
because a VAR($p$) cannot propagate much beyond horizon $p$: uncertainty is then mechanically
underestimated, and the researcher risks becoming confident about the wrong point estimate.
They also stress that truncation affects inference at \emph{all} horizons, including
$h \leq p$.

This has an uncomfortable implication for the exercise being replicated. ACEL's headline
stylised facts are hump-shaped responses peaking roughly one to two years after the shock,
that is at $h \approx 4$ to $8$. With $p=4$, essentially all of those horizons lie beyond the
truncation horizon. The empirical target against which the DSGE is matched therefore lies in
the region where the VAR is extrapolating through an estimated lag polynomial rather than
estimating. I make this visible in Section \ref{sec:results} by marking $h=p$ in the impulse
response figures.

stimano lo stesso estimando, e le LP sono combinazioni di VAR di ordine crescente, quindi
confrontare LP e VAR a lag fissato mette a confronto un modello grande e uno piccolo. Tre o
quattro righe, non di piu'.}

\section{Stochastic singularity and the absence of a VAR representation}
\label{sec:Stochastic}

\subsection{When does a DSGE admit a VAR representation?}

The log-linearised solution of a DSGE model can be written in state-space (or ABCD) form, and
\citet{FVRRS2005} provide a simple condition --- the \emph{Poor Man's Invertibility Condition}
(PMC) --- under which that state-space system can be inverted into a VAR($\infty$) in the
observables. The condition is that the eigenvalues of $A - BD^{-1}C$ lie inside the unit
circle. The notation already reveals the maintained assumption: $D^{-1}$ must exist, so $D$
must be square. In economic terms, the model must have exactly as many structural shocks as
observable variables.

This is not a technicality that can be waved away. When the number of shocks falls short of
the number of observables the system is \emph{stochastically singular}: a $K$-dimensional
process driven by $r < K$ shocks has a spectral density of rank $r$, its Wold representation
is $y_t = \Psi(L)\varepsilon_t$ with $\Psi$ of dimension $K \times r$, and there is no
non-singular $K$-dimensional white noise. The reduced-form covariance matrix $\Sigma_u$ is
singular, $\Sigma_u^{-1}$ does not exist, and a VARMA or VAR($\infty$) representation in the
standard sense is not defined. \citet{Ravenna2007} states the practical implication directly:
when the number of shocks is smaller than the number of observables the system is singular,
``preventing likelihood estimation of the VAR'', and recovering a non-singular VAR
representation requires dropping observables until the two counts coincide.

\subsection{How the literature has dealt with singularity}

\citet{Long_Lag2025} generate artificial data from three well-known DSGE models --- the RBC
models of \citet{ChariKehoeMcGrattan2008} and \citet{CEV2006}, and \citet{SM2007} --- and
estimate VARs on that data across short and long lag choices. Their design is explicit about
the restriction just described: ``invertibility is never a problem; all the models we consider
are fundamental'', and ``all our experiments are based on two-shock models and two-variable
VARs''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~10]{Long_Lag2025}.} The two RBC models satisfy this by
construction. The Smets--Wouters model does not: it carries seven structural shocks, so
confronting it with a two-variable VAR would leave the system singular. Their solution is to
reduce the model, retaining ``only monetary policy and preference shocks'', until the number
of shocks matches the number of variables in the VAR.\footnote{Note the direction of the
adjustment: the binding constraint is the size of the VAR laboratory, which is fixed at two
variables throughout, so squareness is restored by discarding \emph{shocks}, not observables.
Discarding observables is the alternative route, and is the one suggested by
\citet{FVRRS2005} and \citet{Ravenna2007}.} In either direction, the operation is the same:
squareness is imposed so that truncation bias can be studied in isolation from singularity
and invertibility.

There is, however, a strand of the literature arguing that this requirement is far stronger
than the analysis actually needs. \citet{Forni_Gambetti_Sala2016} observe that
``fundamentalness is extremely and unnecessarily restrictive'', because ``in several cases
fundamentalness is not needed. The researcher is often interested in assessing the
transmission mechanisms of a single shock.'' What matters, in their view, ``is not whether we
have fundamentalness or not, but whether we have enough information to get a reasonably good
approximation for the specific shocks of interest''. They also note the cost of insisting on
it: ``if the number of variables in the VAR is smaller than the number of shocks in the DSGE,
fundamentalness cannot hold (and the PMC is not even defined). Since modern DSGE models have
several shocks, small VARs are automatically ruled out. This is unpleasant, since small VARs
are useful tools, especially for short samples.''

This is precisely the position that IRF matching occupies. The strategy does not require the
DSGE to be the data generating process for all observables: it requires the VAR to be
informative about the transmission of the shocks being matched. Section \ref{sec:methodology}
develops this point.

\subsection{The present exercise: singular, and at the parametrisation frontier}

The VAR in \citet{ACEL2011} confronts three structural shocks --- monetary policy, neutral
technology and capital-embodied technology --- with ten observable variables. The system is
therefore stochastically singular, the PMC is not defined for it, and there is no exact
VAR($\infty$) representation of the DSGE that the VAR is being used to estimate. This is the
first respect in which the present exercise departs from \citet{Long_Lag2025}: rather than
restoring squareness by design, I study the consequences of lag truncation in a setting where
singularity is a feature of the model being estimated and cannot be assumed away.

The second departure is one of scale, and it works in the opposite direction to what one might
expect. Because the three data generating processes in \citet{Long_Lag2025} are two-variable
systems, their VARs remain cheaply parametrised even at long lag lengths, and increasing $p$
costs relatively little in degrees of freedom. The ACEL VAR does not enjoy that luxury. With
$K=10$ variables, a constant and $p$ lags, each equation carries $10p+1$ coefficients, and the
sample runs from 1959:Q2 to 2001:Q4, that is $171$ quarterly observations before lags are
consumed. Table \ref{tab:df_budget} reports the resulting budget.

At $p=12$ the system carries $1{,}210$ coefficients, each equation is estimated on $159$
observations with $121$ regressors, and only $38$ degrees of freedom survive. The exercise is
close to the algebraic ceiling: ordinary least squares requires $10p+1 < 171-p$, so no lag
order above $p=15$ is feasible at all, and $p=15$ would leave five degrees of freedom per
equation. Tripling the lag length relative to the ACEL baseline is therefore not a
conservative perturbation --- it is very nearly the largest perturbation the data permit.

This makes the exercise a demanding test of the central claim in \citet{Long_Lag2025}. Their
argument is that longer lags reduce misspecification, that reduced misspecification lowers the
variance of the residuals, and that this variance reduction can offset the imprecision caused
by additional parameters. In a two-variable system the offset has ample room to operate. Here
it does not: the parametrisation grows by a factor of three in a system already large relative
to the sample. If confidence intervals were going to explode anywhere, they would explode here.

They do not. Figure \ref{fig:var_irfs_ci_all_lags} plots the impulse responses to a monetary
policy shock at each lag order together with their own $95$ percent bootstrap confidence

the Phillips curve slope is estimated roughly six times more precisely at $p=12$ than at $p=4$
(Section \ref{sec:results_params}). The variance-reduction mechanism of \citet{Long_Lag2025}
therefore operates even at the parametrisation frontier, which is a considerably more
demanding test than the two-variable systems in which it was originally demonstrated.

\tbd{Quantificare invece di limitarsi a ``visibly narrower'': calcolare la larghezza media
della banda per variabile e lag order, e riportare il rapporto medio $p=12$ contro $p=4$.
Serve una riga di numeri nel testo, non solo il rimando alla figura.}

Two features of how these bands are constructed matter for interpreting them. First, they are
bootstrap bands in which \emph{the entire VAR is re-estimated on each artificial sample}, so
the uncertainty induced by estimating $1{,}210$ coefficients is fully propagated into the
reported intervals rather than being conditioned away. Had the estimator been unstable at
$p=12$, the re-estimated responses would have varied wildly across draws and the bands would
have widened accordingly. Second, draws yielding an explosive companion matrix are discarded
and redrawn, so the bands condition on stationarity.
\tbd{Riportare la frequenza di draw esplosivi (\texttt{count\_var\_expl} in
\texttt{mkimplrnewci.m}) ai tre lag order: e' una diagnostica di stabilita' gratuita e un
lettore attento la chiedera'.}

An obvious objection is that this is simply overfitting: a VAR with $121$ regressors per
equation fits the sample closely, the estimated residuals shrink, and confidence bands
computed from those residuals shrink with them. The objection has to be taken seriously,
because in-sample it cannot be dismissed --- reduced misspecification and overfitting have the
same in-sample signature, namely a smaller residual variance. What can be said here is that
the bootstrap re-estimation described above rules out the mechanical part of the concern,
since parameter uncertainty is not being suppressed. What it cannot rule out is that the
residual variance itself is understated, because the bootstrap resamples from the fitted
residuals of the very model under scrutiny. Distinguishing the two requires evidence that is
not in-sample: this is precisely what \citet{Long_Lag2025} supply, through coverage rates
computed in a controlled environment where the true responses are known, and what motivates
the bias--variance discussion in Section \ref{sec:whylonger}.

\section{The Theoretical Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Inflation inertia and the micro--macro conflict}

\citet{ACEL2011} start from a tension in the New Keynesian literature. To account for inertia
in inflation, standard models require firms to re-optimise prices infrequently --- every six
quarters, and in versions without indexation to past inflation even every two years. This sits
uneasily with the microeconometric evidence, which finds price changes considerably more
frequent. \tbd{Citare Bils and Klenow (2004), Golosov and Lucas (2007), Klenow and Kryvtsov
(2008), che sono i riferimenti di ACEL. Valutare se aggiungere evidenza micro piu' recente.
Da aggiungere a biblio.bib.}

\citet{ACEL2005WP} propose a resolution. In their model inflation is inertial even though
firms re-optimise on average every $1.5$ quarters. Inertia arises not because firms change
prices rarely, but because when they do they change them by a small amount: each firm's
short-run marginal cost is increasing in its own output, which dampens the optimal price
adjustment. The assumption delivering this is that capital is predetermined within the period
and firm-specific, so that factors of production cannot be instantly reallocated across firms.

\subsection{Homogeneous and firm-specific capital}

ACEL conduct the analysis using two versions of the \citet{CEE2005} model: one in which
capital is homogeneous, as in standard formulations, and one in which it is firm-specific.
Once log-linearised, the only difference between the two sets of equilibrium conditions lies
in the equation relating inflation to marginal cost,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\hat{\pi}_t = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\beta\Delta\hat{\pi}_{t+1} + \gamma\hat{s}_t \mid \Omega_t\bigr],
    \label{eq:phillips}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{s}_t$ is economy-wide average real marginal cost in units of the final good, and
the slope $\gamma$ maps into the structural parameters as
\begin{equation}
    \gamma = \frac{(1-\xi_p)(1-\beta\xi_p)}{\xi_p}\,\chi .
    \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
The information set $\Omega_t$ contains the current realisation of the technology shocks but
not that of the monetary policy innovation.

The two models are parametrised in terms of $\gamma$ rather than $\xi_p$. Given the remaining
parameters they are then observationally equivalent with respect to aggregate prices and
quantities, so that no stand need be taken on which version is being estimated. What
distinguishes them is the value of $\xi_p$ that a given $\gamma$ implies.

\subsection{The role of \texorpdfstring{$\chi$}{chi}}

\citet{ACEL2011} establish that $\chi = 1$ in the homogeneous capital model, while in the
firm-specific capital model $\chi$ is a particular non-linear function of the structural
parameters.\footnote{Proposition 1 in \citet{ACEL2011}. \tbd{La derivazione e' in ACEL (2004)
o in Christiano (2004): verificare quale sia il riferimento corretto e aggiungerlo a
biblio.bib. Il PDF di Christiano (2004) e' in ``Letteratura x Tesi''.}}
Two forces push $\chi$ below one, and both work by making a firm move its price \emph{less}
when it is able to reset it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the costlier it is to vary capital utilisation, the steeper the firm's own marginal
    cost curve, and hence the smaller the optimal price adjustment;
    \item the more elastic the firm's demand curve, the larger the fall in output --- and
    therefore in marginal cost --- following a price increase.
\end{itemize}
In short, $\chi$ governs \emph{how much} a firm moves its price and $\xi_p$ governs \emph{how
often}, with implied duration $1/(1-\xi_p)$. Since $\gamma$ is pinned down by aggregate data,
a smaller $\chi$ requires a smaller $\xi_p$: this is the sense in which firm-specific capital
rationalises frequent price re-optimisation.

Two features of equation \eqref{eq:gamma} will matter for the results. First, the mapping from
$\gamma$ to $\xi_p$ is a quadratic that becomes nearly singular as $\gamma \to 0$, so that
small changes in the estimated slope translate into large changes in the implied Calvo
parameter, and normality does not survive the transformation. Second, and more substantively,
ACEL themselves identify the fragility of their own mechanism: ``to the extent that capital
utilization rates can easily be varied, the assumption of firm-specific capital loses its
ability to rationalize low values of $\xi_p$''.\footnote{\citet[][p.~227]{ACEL2011}.} Because
the price markup $\lambda_f$ is calibrated rather than estimated, the cost of varying capital
utilisation $\sigma_a$ is the only estimated parameter entering $\chi$, and therefore the
natural suspect whenever the gap between the two models moves.

Se $\hat\chi \to 1$ passando da $p=4$ a $p=12$, quella diventa la tabella centrale del paper e
la Sezione \ref{sec:results} si riorganizza attorno a essa.}

\section{Econometric Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}



\subsection{Impulse response matching}

Under full-information estimation the DSGE is written in state-space form and the likelihood
of the raw data is evaluated through the Kalman filter. The maintained assumption is that the
model \emph{is} the data generating process: misspecification in any single block contaminates
all parameter estimates, because the criterion weights the entire covariance structure. It
also requires the system to be non-singular, which is why models estimated this way are built
with as many shocks as observables.

IRFME makes a weaker claim. The moments matched are the responses of the observables to the
identified shocks; the remaining dimensions of the innovation space are left unmodelled. The
statement is not ``my model generated the data'' but ``whatever generated the data, the
response to this shock looks like this, and my model should reproduce it''. The estimator
chooses $\theta$ to minimise
\begin{equation}
    \bigl[\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\theta)\bigr]' W \bigl[\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\theta)\bigr],
    \label{eq:irfme}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\Psi}$ collects the VAR-based impulse responses over the matched horizons,
$\Psi(\theta)$ their model counterparts, and $W$ is a weighting matrix.
\tbd{Specificare quali orizzonti e quali variabili ACEL matchano e come e' costruito $W$
(dalla matrice di covarianza delle IRF stimate). Recuperare dal replication package.}

Identification in the VAR is chosen to be consistent with the model. The monetary policy shock
is identified through short-run recursive restrictions: only a subset of contemporaneous
variables enters the information set of the policy rule. The two technology shocks are
identified through long-run restrictions: technology innovations are the only shocks affecting
the level of labour productivity in the long run, and capital-embodied technology shocks are
the only ones affecting the relative price of investment in the long run.
\tbd{Aggiungere Fisher (2006) a biblio.bib, che e' il riferimento seguito da ACEL.} There is
no circularity in using the model to justify the restrictions and the restrictions to estimate
the model, because the restrictions are qualitative and hold across a wide class of models and
parameter values, while the parameters being estimated are quantitative.

\subsection{What the strategy rests on}

The robustness that IRFME buys is robustness to misspecification in the dimensions the
researcher declines to model. It is not robustness to misspecification of the VAR itself. The
strategy has one load-bearing assumption --- that $\hat{\Psi}$ consistently estimates the
population impulse responses --- and lag truncation violates exactly that.

The consequence is worth being precise about. The estimator remains consistent, but for the
pseudo-true parameter value that reconciles the model with the \emph{biased} target. This is
not an inference problem that wider standard errors would address: it is a displaced
objective. Moreover, the identification channel of \citet{Ravenna2007} implies that the damage
is not confined to the moments being matched, since truncation also corrupts the mapping from
reduced-form residuals to structural shocks that produced $\hat{\Psi}$ in the first place.

\tbd{Nota a pie' di pagina da sviluppare: in \citet{CEE2005}, Sezione IV, il polinomio del VAR
stimato entra direttamente nel modello come processo dello shock di politica monetaria. ACEL
stimano invece il blocco stocastico $\zeta_3$ congiuntamente ai parametri strutturali. In
entrambi i casi la struttura dei ritardi del VAR alimenta anche il lato modello, non solo il
bersaglio: cambiando $p$ si cambiano quindi due cose insieme. Verificare sul replication
package come e' implementato in ACEL.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\tbd{Struttura proposta, tre paragrafi.

(1) Cosa faccio e cosa trovo. Replico ACEL arricchendo la struttura dei ritardi del VAR: le
IRF identificate a breve periodo sono relativamente robuste, quelle identificate a lungo no; i
parametri strutturali si spostano in modo sostanziale; il divario tra modello firm-specific e
omogeneo scompare.

(2) Cosa significa. Non ``ACEL avevano torto'', ma che la loro conclusione dipende da una
scelta --- $p=4$ --- che non era percepita come una scelta sostanziale. Il meccanismo e'
interno al loro stesso argomento, via $\sigma_a$ e $\chi$. Piu' in generale: IRFME e'
presentata come robusta alla misspecificazione del modello, ma non e' robusta alla
misspecificazione del VAR, e la seconda passa inosservata perche' tutti controllano la prima.

(3) Cosa resta aperto. Non sostengo che $p=12$ sia il valore corretto: l'esercizio e' di
sensibilita', non di selezione del modello. Estensioni: altri modelli stimati per IRF
matching, e il confronto con le Local Projections alla luce di \citet{Ludwig2026} e
\citet{Plagborg-Moller_Wolf2021}.

Nota per me: le conclusioni sono la parte che sento piu' debole, da rivedere dopo il riscontro
di Efrem.}
