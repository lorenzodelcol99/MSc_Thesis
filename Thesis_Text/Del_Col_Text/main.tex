\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Following \citet{Long_Lag2025} and \citet{Baumeister2025}, I increase the lag length of
the VAR in \citet{ACEL2005WP} to re-estimate their DSGE. Moving from $p=4$ to $p=12$ lags, I
find that both the VAR and DSGE impulse responses, and the implied structural parameters,
change substantially. The Calvo price parameters that distinguish the observationally
equivalent firm-specific and homogeneous capital versions of the model converge to one
another, from $0.34$ against $0.82$ at $p=4$ to $0.89$ against $0.90$ at $p=12$. The implied
duration of price contracts in the firm-specific model rises from $1.5$ to $9.0$ quarters,
equal to that of the homogeneous capital model, re-opening the micro--macro discrepancy in
price stickiness that firm-specific capital was introduced to resolve.

    \vspace{0.3cm}
    \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Truncation Bias, Long-Lag VARs, DSGE Estimation, IRF Minimum Distance , Nominal Rigidities
    \\
    \noindent\textbf{JEL Codes:} C32, C52, E32
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Motivation}
% [target: ~2 pp]

Inflation is persistent; when it appears is typically going to stay for some time, and it is difficult to bring down. 
This is a stylised fact of the data, as can be seen in the post second world war US inflation history in Figure \ref{fig:inflation}.

\FloatBarrier

Many Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models  
are built to explain this phenomena. But standard New Keynesian (NK) models are able to model 
this inflation behaviour only implying implausibly high levels of price stickiness, which are 
in stark contrast with micro-data evidence. \citet{ACEL2011} propose a resolution to this micro-macro conflict. 
They create two different models built on two versions of the \citet{CEE2005} model: 
one with \textit{homogeneous} capital and the other where the capital is \textit{firm-specific}.
\\

One of the important goals of monetary business cycle research is to explain the empirical evidence 
on the impact of economic shocks on macroeconomic variables.
Because the systematic component of policy is endogenous, identifying the causal effect 
of monetary policy shocks requires isolating the non-systematic component, the monetary policy 
shock, even though it is small and infrequent \cite{Ravenna2007,Aruoba_Drechsel2024_MP_LLM}. 


A large class of estimated DSGE models is taken to the data through this channel.
Impulse Response Function Matching Estimation (IRFME) is a limited-information strategy that chooses
structural parameters to minimise the distance between the impulse responses implied by the
model and those estimated from an identified VAR \citep{CEE2005, ACEL2011,
Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}. The strategy is attractive precisely because it is partial:
the researcher takes a stand on the identification of a subset of shocks, and remains
agnostic about the remaining dimensions of the data.
\\

That attractiveness rests entirely on one maintained assumption: that the VAR delivers
\emph{consistent} estimates of the moments being matched. If the reduced-form VAR adequately
captures the data generating process, impulse responses are optimal at all horizons; if the
VAR is misspecified, the specification error propagates to the structural statistics and
compounds at each horizon \citep{Ramey2016, Canova_Fosso2025}
\cite{Ravenna2007} "The bias introduced by truncation can lead to bias in the identification 
of the structural shock."
\citet[][footnote 10, p.~3]{ACEL2011} say as much
themselves: ``the quality of our estimation strategy depends on the ability of identified
VARs to generate reliable estimates of the dynamic response of economic variables to
shocks''.
\\

Since a VAR($\infty$) cannot be estimated on a finite sample, researchers truncate. The
resulting VAR($p$) is misspecified, and increasingly so the smaller is $p$
\citep{Long_Lag2025, Humble_VARs_2026, Ravenna2007}. 
\cite{Ravenna2007} "the problem facing economists is whether the number of lags $p$ to be 
included is reasonable given the length of economics time series over which VARs are
estimated. When estimating a VAR consistent with DSGE business cycle models it is
standard to assume that including few lags is sufficient to provide a reasonable
approximation to the exact infinite order VAR representation!
\citet{Long_Lag2025} show that the
conventional objection to longer lags, that the additional parametrisation makes
confidence intervals prohibitively wide, is incomplete, because reducing misspecification
reduces not only bias but also variance. Their conclusion is addressed explicitly to applied
researchers: ``it is up to the researcher to show that her structural results are robust to
significant increases in the lag length, far beyond the lag lengths typically considered in
current practice''.


\subsection{Contribution and Results}
% [target: ~1 pp]

This paper asks what lag truncation does to \emph{structural} estimates, not merely to
impulse responses. I replicate \citet{ACEL2011} --- henceforth ACEL --- and re-estimate their
DSGE by IRF matching against VARs of increasing lag order.

The contribution is twofold. First, relative to \citet{Long_Lag2025}, I move the question from
the reduced form to the structural parameters. Second, and more specifically, I do so in a
setting their design deliberately excludes. \citet{Long_Lag2025} state that ``all our
experiments are based on two-shock models and two-variable VARs'', and that where a candidate
model does not fit that framework --- Smets and Wouters --- ``we consider the model with only
monetary policy and preference shocks''.\footnote{\citep[p.~10]{Long_Lag2025}. They also note
that ``invertibility is never a problem; all the models we consider are fundamental''.} Their
data generating processes are square and fundamental by construction, so that truncation bias
is isolated from singularity and invertibility. ACEL's model is not: it confronts three
structural shocks with a ten-variable VAR.
\tbd{Verificare che nessun altro abbia gia' fatto questo esercizio su un modello
stocasticamente singolare. Se confermato, e' il claim centrale.}

My main results are the following. \emph{First}, the impulse responses to a monetary policy
shock retain their sign and broad shape across lag orders, but the humps peak substantially
later as $p$ increases, and the point estimates remain inside the VAR(4) confidence bands.
\emph{Second}, the responses to the two technology shocks --- identified with long-run rather
than short-run restrictions --- do not: several fall outside the baseline confidence set. This
asymmetry is what \citet{Ravenna2007} predicts, since long-run restrictions require the sum of
the entire lag polynomial and truncation error accumulates in exactly that object.
\emph{Third}, the structural estimates move. The Phillips curve slope falls from $0.0402$ to
$0.0129$ and becomes precisely estimated only at $p=12$; the cost of varying capital
utilisation collapses from $1.99$ to $0.004$; the investment adjustment cost roughly doubles.
\emph{Fourth}, and as a consequence, the implied Calvo price parameters of the firm-specific
and homogeneous capital models converge, from $0.34$ against $0.82$ to $0.89$ against $0.90$.
The implied duration of price contracts in the firm-specific model rises from $1.5$ to $9.0$
quarters.

The last result overturns ACEL's central conclusion. Their claim is that only the
firm-specific capital model reconciles macro evidence on inflation inertia with micro evidence
on the frequency of price adjustment. Under a richer lag structure that reconciliation
disappears. The channel is transparent and internal to their own argument: ACEL note that
``to the extent that capital utilization rates can easily be varied, the assumption of
firm-specific capital loses its ability to rationalize low values of
$\xi_p$''.\footnote{\citep[p.~227]{ACEL2011}.} Long-lag VARs push the estimate of $\sigma_a$
precisely into that region.

\section{The Theoretical Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Inflation inertia and the micro--macro conflict}

\citet{ACEL2011} start from a tension in the New Keynesian literature. To account for inertia
in inflation, standard models require firms to re-optimise prices infrequently; every six
quarters, and in versions without indexation to past inflation even every two years. This sits
uneasily with the microeconometric evidence, which finds price changes considerably more
frequent \cite{BilsKlenow2004, GolosovLucas2007, KlenowKryvtsov2008}.
% TBD: valutare se aggiungere evidenza micro piu' recente oltre a questi tre riferimenti di ACEL.
\\

\citet{ACEL2005WP}\footnote{The replication code made available from the authors is NBER working paper from 2005. Hence, I replicate and refer to those results.} 
propose a resolution. In their model inflation is inertial even though
firms re-optimise on average every $1.5$ quarters. Inertia arises not because firms change
prices rarely, but because when they do it, they change them by a small amount: each firm's
short-run marginal cost is increasing in its own output, which dampens the optimal price
adjustment. The underlying assumption that drives this result is that capital is predetermined within the period
and firm-specific, so that factor of production cannot be instantly and costlessly reallocated across firms.

\subsection{Homogeneous and firm-specific capital}
\label{sec:2.2homog_bs_firm-spec_capital}

\citet{ACEL2011} build on the \citet{CEE2005} model, adding the two technology shocks, and
construct two versions of it: one in which capital is homogeneous, as in standard
formulations, and one in which it is firm-specific.

Once log-linearised, the only difference between the two sets of equilibrium conditions lies
in the equation relating inflation $\pi_t$ to marginal cost, the New Keynesian Phillips curve,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\hat{\pi}_t = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\beta\Delta\hat{\pi}_{t+1} + \gamma\hat{s}_t \mid \Omega_t\bigr],
    \label{eq:phillips}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{s}_t$ is economy-wide average real marginal cost in units of the final good, and
the slope $\gamma$ maps into the structural parameters as
\begin{equation}
    \gamma = \frac{(1-\xi_p)(1-\beta\xi_p)}{\xi_p}\,\chi .
    \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
The information set $\Omega_t$ contains the current realisation of the technology shocks but
not that of the monetary policy innovation. Here $\xi_p$ is the Calvo price stickiness
parameter, so that a fraction $1-\xi_p$ of firms reset their price each quarter and the
implied duration of a price contract is $D = 1/(1-\xi_p)$.

Both models are parametrised in terms of $\gamma$ rather than $\xi_p$. Given the remaining
parameters they are then observationally equivalent with respect to aggregate prices and
quantities, so that no stand need be taken on which version is being estimated. What
distinguishes them is the value of $\xi_p$ that a given $\gamma$ implies, and that in turn
depends on $\chi$.
\\

The real rigidity parameter $\chi$ measures the strategic complementarity in price setting of
\citet{BallRomer1990}. It works as an attenuation factor, takes values in $(0,1]$, and the
closer it lies to zero the stronger the attenuation: a firm that is able to reset its price
moves it by less, for a given movement in economy-wide marginal cost. In the
\textit{homogeneous} capital model $\chi$ is set to be one and the attenuation is absent. In the
\textit{firm-specific} model $\chi$ is a non-linear function of the structural parameters,
namely of the calibrated $[\alpha, \beta, \delta, \lambda_f]$, respectively the capital share
in the Cobb-Douglas production function, the discount factor, the depreciation rate and the
average price markup, and of two estimated parameters, $[\sigma_a, S'']$, 
capital utilisation cost and the investment adjustment cost respectively.
\\

The investment adjustment cost $S''$ enters $\chi$ too, but through a different channel: 
it governs the cost of changing the flow of new investment, which shapes how quickly the 
firm's own capital stock accumulates in future periods, not how much capital it can access this quarter. 
Since $\chi$ is pinned down mainly by the within-period margin that $\sigma_a$ governs, $S''$ 
has correspondingly little quantitative bearing on it.

The role of capital utilisation cost $\sigma_a$ is best understood by following what happens after an unexpected,
positive demand shock. Aggregate demand rises within the quarter and firms have to produce
more. Hours expand first, and wages are themselves set on a Calvo scheme, so only the
$1-\xi_w$ of households that can reoptimise choose a new wage, while the rest adjust only
mechanically through indexation. The aggregate nominal wage therefore rises, and the real
wage with it, but by little. Capital is another matter, and it is here that the two models
part company.
\\

Under homogeneous capital there is a competitive economy-wide market for capital services.
In the aggregate the capital stock is predetermined within the quarter, so the additional
services can only come from higher utilisation, and how far the rental rate must rise to call
them forth is governed by $\sigma_a$. That much is true in both models, and it is not what
separates them. But in the homogeneous capital model each takes the rental rate as given and hires as many capital services as it
wants at it, since capital can be moved across firms within the period. Any single firm's
capital stock is therefore not predetermined, even though the economy's is.
\citet[p.~2]{ACEL2005WP} put the assumption plainly: ``a firm's capital stock is not
pre-determined and all factors of production, including capital, can be instantaneously
transferred across firms, without any cost, in perfectly competitive markets''. Both factors
are then available at prices the firm does not influence, so its marginal cost is the same as
every other firm's and does not depend on how much it produces itself. That is what
$\chi = 1$ records.
\\

Under the firm-specific capital model there is no market for capital services. The firm is left with
the capital it already owns, which cannot be reallocated within the period, so the only way
to obtain more capital services is to run its own stock harder. It would be willing to pay for additional services, but there is no market on which to buy
them, and $\sigma_a$ governs how much raising utilisation costs it instead. When $\sigma_a$ is high that margin is expensive, so
most of the additional output has to come from adding hours to a quantity of capital services
that barely moves. The marginal product of labour falls as the fixed factor binds, and the
firm's own marginal cost rises with its own output.
\\

This changes the decision of the optimiser firms that can reset their price. Economy-wide
marginal cost has risen, so those firms wish to raise prices. But a firm that raises its
price by more than the others sees its relative price rise, its demand fall, and its own
output fall with it, and under firm-specific capital a lower own output means a lower own
marginal cost, which argues for a smaller increase in the first place. Having internalised
that producing more is what raises its costs, the firm moves its price by less than it would
in the absence of this real rigidity, the more so the steeper its own marginal cost curve and
the more elastic the demand it faces, since a given price increase then costs it more output.
Inflation responds less to a given movement in economy-wide marginal cost, and this is
precisely what a $\chi$ below one means.
\\

It is worth being precise about what is doing the work here, because $\sigma_a$ is a single
parameter, estimated once, which does not take different values across the two models. What
differs is whether it reaches $\chi$ at all. Under homogeneous capital the market for capital
services severs the link between a firm's own output and its own marginal cost, and
$\chi = 1$ whatever $\sigma_a$ happens to be. Under firm-specific capital the firm is tied to
the capital it already owns, and $\sigma_a$ decides how binding that tie is.
\\

The limiting case makes the point. If $\sigma_a$ is close to zero, utilisation is almost free
to vary, so a firm tied to its own capital can still draw from it whatever volume of capital
services it needs, at negligible cost. Owning the capital rather than renting it then makes
no practical difference: the firm's own marginal cost is as flat as it would be with a market
for capital services, $\chi$ converges to one, and the firm-specific model collapses onto the
homogeneous one. Firm specificity is an assumption about ownership, but its economic content
comes entirely from the cost of varying utilisation, and without that cost the constraint
exists in name only.
\\

Since $\chi$ is decreasing in $\sigma_a$ and $\gamma$ is proportional to $\chi$, a larger $\sigma_a$
lowers $\gamma$ for any given $\xi_p$. Reading the Phillips curve slope $\gamma$, equation \eqref{eq:gamma}, 
in the other direction is what matters for estimation, as $\gamma$ is estimated from the data.
The term $(1-\xi_p)(1-\beta\xi_p)/\xi_p$ is decreasing in $\xi_p$, so holding $\gamma$ fixed, a smaller
$\chi$ must be offset by a larger value of that term, which requires a smaller $\xi_p$. For a
given slope of the Phillips curve, then, $\xi_p$ is a decreasing function of $\sigma_a$.
\\

This is what resolves the micro macro price conflict. The
empirical literature finds the Phillips curve to be flat, that is $\gamma$ small. In the
homogeneous capital model $\chi = 1$, so a small $\gamma$ can only be delivered by pushing
$\xi_p$ close to one, and the implied duration $D = 1/(1-\xi_p)$ becomes long enough to sit
in open conflict with the micro literature evidence. In the firm-specific model the same
small $\gamma$ is delivered instead by a $\chi$ well below one, which leaves room for a much
lower $\xi_p$. Inflation is inertial not because firms reset their prices rarely, but because
when they do they move them by little. This is how \citet{ACEL2005WP} obtain an average
duration of $1.5$ quarters and still match the aggregate evidence on inflation.

The resolution, however, rests on a single estimated parameter, capital utilisation cost $\sigma_a$, and 
the authors acknowledge the fragility themselves: ``to the extent that capital utilization rates can
easily be varied, the assumption of firm-specific capital loses its ability to rationalize
low values of $\xi_p$'' \citep[p.~227]{ACEL2011}.

\subsection{DSGE estimation via IRF matching}
Theoretical models are useful to economists and policymakers only if they are able to reproduce important
features of the observed data. Which is a property that critically depends on the values that model's 
parameters have attached, and also on how the researcher decide to estimate them \cite{Theodoridis2011}.

\cite{ACEL2005WP} following \cite{RotembergWoodford1997, CEE2005, Iacoviello2005, BoivinGiannoni2006},
use a limited information approach, an IRF matching estimator, 
this procedure select and estimate the model's structural parameters that produce the DSGE IRFs, which
minimises the distance with IRFs created from the SVAR. 

Using an IRFME implies that the researcher is focusing to match on some limited aspects of the DSGE \cite{Giacomini2013}; 
it is choosing an identification strategy for the reduced form VAR in a way that is consistent with the key determinants of the DSGE.
The VAR and theoretical IRFs are identified by restrictions implied by the DSGE. Which means that the DSGE 
admits a structural VAR representation, in this way, sample moments of the VAR are informative for the 
theoretical model's structural parameters \cite{Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}.

\citet{Theodoridis2011}, following \citet{Canova1994, Canova2007}, notes that many DSGE economists prefer to use 
partial information estimators rather than full information ones, like maximum likelihood, because they 
prefer estimates that minimise the economic distance rather than statistical fit criteria.
Because structural IRF matching estimates deliver selected DSGE moments-statistic as close as possible to 
those observed in the data.
The economist can assess how well the estimated model replicates these targets allowing the researcher 
to identify possible modelling weaknesses and to infer useful economic conclusions.
\\

The authors use a medium-scale VAR with four lags, ten quarterly series and three structural shocks.
The monetary policy shock is identified with a short-run recursive Cholesky restriction following \citet{CEE2005}.
The two technology shocks, neutral and capital embodied, are identified instead with
long-run restrictions, following \citet{Fisher2006}, which builds on \citet{Shapiro_Watson88}.
As summarised by \citet{Hall_Inoue_Nason_Rossi2012}, the restriction imposed by
\citet{ACEL2011} is that the two technology shocks are the only shocks allowed to affect the
level of labour productivity in the long run; among them, only the capital embodied shock is
allowed to affect the relative price of investment in the long run. The VAR is identified so
that its impulse responses satisfy these restrictions, and the resulting responses are then
matched to their model counterparts to estimate the structural parameters.
\\

Although the model is estimated from the responses to three shocks, the question that
motivates \citet{ACEL2011} is a monetary one. What the paper sets out to explain is why
inflation responds so slowly to a monetary policy shock while firms in the micro data change
prices often, and the resolution runs entirely through $\chi$, which is governed by the cost of
varying capital utilisation.

That cost is a statement about what happens to a firm's marginal cost when demand for its
output moves while its capital stock is fixed, and a monetary policy shock is exactly that
experiment. It shifts demand within the quarter, technology is unchanged, and the capital stock
is predetermined, so the joint response of utilisation, hours and inflation is close to a
direct reading of Capacity utilisation cost $\sigma_a$. 

Capacity utilisation is itself one of the ten series in the
system, so this is not an indirect inference. A technology shock is a poorer experiment for the
same parameter, because it moves the production function and the desired quantity of capital
services at once, and what the utilisation response reveals is then a mixture of the two.

This is not to say that the technology shocks are decorative. The DSGE carries three structural
disturbances and the estimator uses all of them: they carry information about the investment
adjustment cost and about the parameters governing the propagation of supply-side disturbances,
and without them several parameters would be poorly pinned down. But they are not where the
micro--macro conflict is settled. It is settled by $\sigma_a$, and $\sigma_a$ is read off the
response to a monetary policy shock.
\\

The analysis that follows in this work therefore centres on the monetary policy shock.
If $\sigma_a$ turns
out to be sensitive to the VAR's specification, so is the reconciliation \citet{ACEL2011}
propose, and the sensitivity comes from the monetary policy block, the part of the model this
paper is really about.

The two technology shocks are nonetheless reported and discussed throughout, rather than set
aside. They are identified in a different way, through restrictions on their effects in the
long run rather than on their behaviour within the quarter, and that difference will prove
informative in its own right.

\subsection{SVAR and IRF minimum distance}
The SVAR can be recovered by imposing structure on the reduced form (RF),
\begin{equation}
    B_0^{-1}B_0Y_t = B_0^{-1}B_1Y_{t-1} + \dots + B_0^{-1}B_pY_{t-p} + B_0^{-1}w_t,
\end{equation}
which matches the RF-VAR term by term once $A_i = B_0^{-1}B_i$ for each lag $i$ and
\begin{equation}
    u_t = B_0^{-1}w_t .
    \label{eq:u-w-map}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:u-w-map} is the bridge between the two representations: the
reduced-form residuals are a linear combination of the structural shocks. Taking
variances on both sides,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_u = B_0^{-1}\Sigma_w\bigl(B_0^{-1}\bigr)' .
    \label{eq:sigma-map}
\end{equation}
$\Sigma_w$ is the variance-covariance matrix of the structural shocks $w_t$. These
are assumed mutually orthogonal and normalised to unit variance, so $\Sigma_w = I_K$
by construction. This is a normalisation, not an assumption about the economy: what
is estimated is not the scale of each shock, but how a given shock propagates through
the system, which is entirely encoded in $B_0^{-1}$.

$Y_t$ is the ten-dimensional vector of series used by \citet{ACEL2011},
\begin{equation}
    Y_t =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \Delta\ln(\text{relative price of investment}_t) \\
        \Delta\ln(\text{GDP}_t/\text{Hours}_t) \\
        \Delta\ln(\text{GDP deflator}_t) \\
        \text{Capacity utilisation}_t \\
        \ln(\text{Hours}_t) \\
        \ln(\text{GDP}_t/\text{Hours}_t) - \ln(W_t/P_t) \\
        \ln(C_t/\text{GDP}_t) \\
        \ln(I_t/\text{GDP}_t) \\
        \text{Federal funds rate}_t \\
        \ln(\text{GDP deflator}_t) + \ln(\text{GDP}_t) - \ln(\text{MZM}_t)
    \end{pmatrix} .
    \label{eq:Yt}
\end{equation}
All the variables are quarterly series from the 1959Q2 - 2001Q4 period, more details on data 
are described in Appendix \ref{app:data}.

Identification combines a short-run and a long-run restriction. The full derivation
is in Appendix \ref{app:identification}.

These restrictions identify three shocks out of ten. IRFME matches the IRFs of the three identified shocks, 
not on those not identified.

\subsection{Estimating the Parameters of the Equilibrium Model}
Using \cite{ACEL2011} notation, let $\zeta$ denote the structural model parameters that are aimed to be estimated, and $\Psi(\zeta)$ being the mapping 
from the structural parameters $\zeta$ to the model IRFs. The SVAR IRFs estimates are denoted as $\hat{\Psi}$.

The IRFME then can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\zeta} = \underset{\zeta}{\arg\min} \, (\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\zeta))' V^{-1} (\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\zeta))
    \label{eq:irfme}
\end{equation}
The IRF matching is done horizon by horizon: the estimator compares the VAR
point estimate of each impulse response at each of the first $h$ horizons; being $h=20$ in \cite{ACEL2011} work, excluding
those set to zero by identification, against the model-implied counterpart. 
Here, V is a diagonal matrix composed by the sample variances along the diagonal $\hat{\Psi}$.
Those variances, compose the confidence intervals around the point estimates of the SVAR IRFs.
With this choice of V, the estimated model's structural parameters $\hat{\zeta}$ are the value 
of ${\zeta}$ that ensures that the $\hat{\Psi}$ DSGE's IRFs lies as much as possible inside the VAR IRFs' confidence intervals.
Standard errors of the DSGE's structural parameters $\hat{\zeta}$ are computed using the Delta method, 
more detail in the Appendix \ref{app:delta_method}

The minimum distance estimator relies on the ability of the VAR to estimate the IRFs.
\textit{"if the VAR is misspecified, the specification errors will be computed at each horizon"}
point the attention on misspecification concerns in general VAR practice \citep[p.~83]{Ramey2016}.
This is clear from the authors themselves, who claim \textit{"The quality of our strategy (IRF matching)
depends on the ability of identified VARs to generate reliable estimates of the
dynamic response of economic variables to shocks"} \citep[p.~3, fn.~10]{ACEL2011}.

\section{Truncation Bias and the Case for Long-Lag VARs}
\label{sec:truncation}

Many DSGE's log linearised solution can be mapped in State Space representation, and then, under some assumption
can be re-written as VAR($\infty$) \cite{FVRRSS2007}. Standard lag choice by SVAR practitioner is to 
use forecasting information criteria (IC) such as Akaike (AIC) or Hannan-Quinn (HQ) and Schwartz (SW), but the most common choice is 
use conventions; that is, 4 lags for quarterly data and 12 for monthly data.
\\

If a DSGE is estimated using an IRF matching technique, using a short lag VAR it might be problematic
as the DSGE is estimated by a biased by lag truncation VAR.
Truncation Bias is a type of misspecification that arises when the VAR lag order is shorter than that of its DGP.
This type of misspecification might be a potential problem for structural inference.

VARs are typically small systems with few lags often interpreted as models of the entire economy, 
IRFs are highly sensitive to variable and lag choice \cite{Humble_VARs_2026,Long_Lag2025}.


\subsection{Illustration of the problem: Truncation Bias}

Following the example used by \citet{Long_Lag2025}, consider a data generating process in which $y_t$ depends on
two exogenous variables,
\begin{equation}
    y_t = x_{1t}\beta_1 + x_{2t}\beta_2 + \epsilon_t .
    \label{eq:dgp}
\end{equation}
Estimating the relationship while omitting $x_{2t}$,
\begin{equation}
    y_t = x_{1t} b_1 + e_t ,
    \label{eq:omitted}
\end{equation}
Equation \ref{eq:omitted} delivers a biased point estimate unless the regressors are orthogonal, that is 
$\mathbb{E}(b_1)\neq\beta_1$, and an upward-biased residual variance, $s^2 > \sigma^2$. Both
distortions matter here, and the second is the one usually forgotten.

The same logic carries over to a VAR. Let
\begin{equation}
    Y_t = A_1 Y_{t-1} + \dots + A_p Y_{t-p} + u_t,
    \qquad \mathbb{E}[u_t u_t'] = \Sigma_u ,
    \label{eq:var}
\end{equation}
with 
\begin{equation}
    A(L) = A_1 L + \dots + A_p L^p
\end{equation} 
where $p \ll p^{*}$, where $p^{*}$ denotes the true lag order.
\\

Truncation biases both the reduced-form autoregressive coefficients $A(L)$ and the reduced-form
covariance matrix $\Sigma_u$.
Since impulse responses are a function of both, they inherit the bias.
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IRF} = f\bigl(A(L), \Sigma_u\bigr),
    \label{eq:irf-f}
\end{equation}

Writing the impulse responses this way suggests that $\Sigma_u$ enters structural analysis on
its own. It does not: $\Sigma_u$ is only ever used through a further mapping into the
structural shocks, via the impact matrix $B_0$, and whether that mapping inherits the
truncation bias just described depends entirely on how the shock of interest is identified.

\subsection{Identification Bias}
\label{sec:identification_bias}

\citet{Ravenna2007} finds two channels through which lag truncation operates. The first being \textit{pure
truncation} bias the one described in the section above.
The second he calls it \textit{identification bias}, and works through the fact that
impulse responses are not read off the reduced form alone, they also require the
map from reduced form innovations to structural shocks, and that map
has to be estimated as well.

Ravenna's identification bias arises when the identifying restrictions are correct, 
so it does not originate in mistaken identification assumptions, 
and it arises in population, so it is not an artefact of small samples.

Whenever the estimated autoregressive coefficients enter the identifying map's
computation, a biased $\hat{B}(L)$ delivers a biased $\hat{B}_0$, and the object the researcher
labels a structural shock is not the shock the identifying assumption defines.
\\

Whether the second channel is present, therefore reduces to a single question: do the estimated autoregressive
coefficients enter the computation of the identifying map?
\\

Under a recursive short run scheme they do not. The identifying restrictions are zero restrictions
on contemporaneous relations, so $B_0$ is recovered from $\hat{\Sigma}_u$ alone, and the lag polynomial plays no part in it. This does not make the recursive
scheme immune to pure truncation, since $\hat{\Sigma}_u$ is itself inflated by the omitted dynamics and
the propagation is distorted as it is for everyone else. The short run Cholesky scheme is not affected to the second
channel, identification bias, because the restriction is a statement about the contemporaneous covariance of whatever
innovations the estimated VAR produces, and it retains that meaning at any lag order.
\\

Under a long run scheme they do. Long run identification is known to be delicate for several
reasons, of which truncation is only one \cite{Faust_Leeper1997, Erceg_Guerrieri_Gust2005,
ChariKehoeMcGrattan2008, CEV2007}. The mechanism at work here is clearest in the formulation of
\citet{BlanchardQuah1989}, where the identifying map is obtained by factorising the long run
covariance matrix
\begin{equation}
    [I - B(1)]^{-1}\,\Sigma_u\,\bigl\{[I - B(1)]^{-1}\bigr\}',
    \qquad B(1) = \sum_{j=1}^{p} B_j ,
    \label{eq:lr-map}
\end{equation}
so that the entire lag polynomial evaluated at unity enters the identification directly. A VAR($p$)
does not estimate that sum consistently when the true representation is of infinite order, and the
restriction is therefore imposed on the wrong object.

\citet{ACEL2011} impose the same kind of restriction by a different route, following
\citet{Fisher2006}: instead of rotating an estimated matrix after the fact, they build the
restriction into the estimating equation and read the identified shock off as a regression residual
(mechanics in Appendix \ref{app:identification}). Either way, the restriction binds on the sum of
the lag polynomial the VAR actually estimates, which runs only to $p$, not on the sum over the true
$p^{*}>p$ lags it is meant to capture. The restriction is anchored to the wrong object, and the shock
it delivers is the wrong linear combination of reduced form innovations.
\\

This has a consequence for where the bias may appear. A long
run restriction says nothing about any finite horizon, impact included. It fixes the cumulated
effect and leaves every response along the way to be implied by the estimated dynamics.
The long run restriction is therefore not a constraint on the response at a given horizon: 
it is the rule by which the shock is isolated from the reduced form innovations, 
and it is on that rule that truncation acts. Applied to a truncated polynomial, the identifying restriction 
can isolate a different combination from the intended one. If the shock itself 
turns out to be a different one, the bias is no longer bound to accumulate with 
the horizon: it could already be present at the first period, unlike pure truncation bias 
which builds up over time.
\\

The two channels leave different traces: \textit{pure truncation} bias
distorts propagation, so its mark accumulates with the horizon, while \textit{identification} bias distorts
the shock, so its trace could be visible already on impact.

The VAR of \citet{ACEL2011} carries both kinds of restriction at once, which makes it an unusually
clean setting in which to look for both the channels.
\\

In essence, both the channels in which truncation bias potentially operates can be 
closed or mitigated by simply increasing the lag length of the VAR.
Applied practice has largely resisted that response. Even where long-lag
VARs are known to have good asymptotic properties \cite{Plagborg-Moller_Wolf2021},
they are widely treated as unworkable in finite samples due to high variance concerns: 
more lags mean more parameters, fewer degrees of freedom, and confidence intervals 
presumed to widen accordingly. That presumption is part of why a recent literature 
prefers Local Projections (LP) to VARs outright \cite{Li_PlagborgMoller_Wolf2024, 
Montiel-Olea_Plagborg-Moller_Qian_Wolf2025}.
\\

\cite{Long_Lag2025} explore the trivial solution to short lag VARs, that is, dramatic increase in the 
lag length.

\subsection{Long-Lag VARs}
\label{sec:long-lag_VARs}
Their central point is that standard intuition that, increasing lag length increase uncertainty around 
estimates given the increased parametrisation is just a portion of the story, 
\textit{"in face of misspecification due to lag truncation, increasing the lag 
length can actually reduce uncertainty"} \citep[p.~2]{Long_Lag2025}.

As the dynamic structure increases, lag truncation decrease, consequently misspecification reduces.
Misspecification reduction has a double effect, the first being the well-known bias reduction, while 
the second is the variance reduction that works against the imprecision coming from the increased parametrisation.
This trade off it applies to all truncated VARs, no matter what the identification strategy is.
\\

To reveal Long-Lag VARs properties \cite{Long_Lag2025} generate data, with a Monte Carlo, of realistic samples size 
from three DSGE.
The three DSGE are \cite{ChariKehoeMcGrattan2008}, \cite{CEV2007} and \cite{SW2007}.

This Monte Carlo simulation is conducted under the assumptions that the identification restriction is correct.
And the DSGE are fundamental in the sense of \cite{FVRRSS2007}, so invertibility is never a problem.
Under these hypothesis these models have an exact VAR($\infty$) representation.

With the Monte Carlo they generate $1000$ datasets of realistic macro sample size for quarterly data, $T = 200$,
and they estimate the bivariate VARs \footnote{For \cite{SW2007}, they reduce the number of variables from 7 to 2 
to maintain consistency with the other two exercises. They keep GDP-growth and interest rates.}
representations of the DSGE choosing different increasing the lag choice.
When comparing VARs of different lag-length, they restrict the sample of effective observations as $T = 170$ 
ensuring that lag initialisation does not affect sample size.
Uncertainty bands are computed as in \citep{Sims_Zha1999, Canova2007, Uhlig2005}, in this sense 
it is used a the Normal-Inverse Wishart prior, which have good approximation to frequentist bootstrapped intervals.
It does not weight differently short vs. long lags as would do other priors like the Minnesota one.

\subsection{Long-Lag Benefits}
\label{sec:long-lag_benefits}
\cite{Long_Lag2025} analyse how the truncated VARs of the three DSGE with VAR($\infty$) 
representation behave. 
The results are much in favor for long-lag VARs.
The variance-reducing effect of lower misspecification often dominates the penalty of increased parametrisation.
In a nutshell, Long-Lag VARs, reduce truncation bias, have more 
precise inference, reduced Mean Squared Error (MSE) and better coverage.

Short-lag VARs have maximum uncertainty for horizons where uncertainty is not mechanically low.
For longer horizons, short-lag VARs have minimum CI width, this because a VAR($p$) cannot propagate much 
beyond horizon $p$, VARs with lag length extrapolate impulse responses for 
horizons $h > p$ from the first $p$ autocovariances \cite{Baumeister2025}. 
Therefore uncertainty cannot propagate much beyond that horizon either.

Uncertainty does not explode with the lag length increase; instead for VARs with very long lags, confidence bands 
are roughly in the same ballpark as those of short-lag VARs.


Long-Lag VARs have comparable coverage rates and comparable MSE than short-lag VARs, combined with 
the tendency to produce smaller biases, long-lag VARs have favorable properties compared to standard short-lag VARs.

\cite{Long_Lag2025} leverage to the above mentioned Monte Carlo simulation proposing an exercise that decompose the uncertainty effects creating hypothetical IRFs
that are composed by long-lag estimates of the point estimates, while using short-lag estimates of variance covariance matrix 
obtaining an $IRF = f(B_{30}(L), \Sigma_4)$. Such IRFs can be interpreted as isolating the effect of increased parametrisation.

The standard intuition would suggest that long-lag length makes the entire distribution shift outward,
through the additional uncertainty created by the strong increase of parameters.
In their exercise, they find that the increased parametrisation does not invariably increase uncertainty, which is at odds both with standard intuition (fewer degrees of freedom) and with the well known omitted variable result.

Standard intuition on increased parametrisation is partially correct and obviously part of the story, but 
\cite{Long_Lag2025} finds that misspecification reduction tends to have substantial variance effects.

\subsection{Choosing the Lag Length}

Macroeconomists almost never have the luxury of knowing the true DGP, unlike in the Monte
Carlo exercise just reviewed, so point estimates cannot be checked against a known truth the
way \citet{Long_Lag2025} do in simulation. 

What can be checked instead is robustness: whether
the structural conclusions hold up as the lag length increases substantially, or shift as $p$
grows. Stability across lag orders is the closest a researcher can come to the reassurance the
Monte Carlo evidence provides, which is why \citet{Baumeister2025}, and \citet{Long_Lag2025} themselves both
ask whether long-lag VARs remain a viable methodology in sample data, revisiting well known
empirical studies rather than relying on simulation alone: \citet{Long_Lag2025} return to
\citet{Gali1999}, where the evidence against RBC models in favour of New Keynesian ones
weakens with longer lags; to \citet{Chang_Hong2006}, whose central result is similarly less
strong; and to \citet{MirandaAgrippinoRicco2021}, where longer lags change the estimated
duration of the monetary policy shock's impact.
\\

This robustness check is something a researcher cannot choose to skip, for two reasons. The first
applies whenever the economic question is itself about the long run: a VAR($p$) cannot
propagate a response, or say anything informative, beyond horizon $p$, so a long-run question
mechanically requires a long-lag VAR. \citet{Baumeister2025} illustrate the point directly:
re-estimating the BVAR of \citet{Jorda2024long_run_mp} with 60 monthly lags eliminate its
conclusions on the long-run effects of monetary policy. \citet{Antolin-Diaz_Surico2025} build
the same requirement into their design from the outset, using $p=h$ lags to draw structural
inference at horizon $h$.
\\

The second feature applies even when the question is not about the long run at all, and it is
the point I want to stress here. When the DSGE is estimated by IRF matching, the estimator
does not target a single horizon: it minimises the distance between VAR and DSGE impulse
responses jointly over $h$ periods, up to $h=20$ in \citet{ACEL2011}. If the VAR misspecifies
the dynamics it is asked to estimate, that misspecification is present at every one of those
horizons, and the estimator is matched to an object that is biased throughout the window, not
only near its end. \citet{Antolin-Diaz_Surico2025} set $p=h$ because their question is
explicitly about the long run; what this thesis adds is that under IRF matching the same
requirement follows even when it is not, because the estimator's own objective function
reaches out to horizon $h$ regardless of what the underlying economic question asks about. A
researcher whose question is genuinely about short-run dynamics is therefore not exempt from
the choice of $p$: as long as the estimation strategy is IRF matching, $p$ has to be chosen
with $h$ in mind, not only with the horizon of the substantive question.
\\

The mismatch can equally be resolved from the other side, by lowering $h$: twenty quarters, five years, is a long horizon for a question that is meant to be about
the short run, and a shorter matching window asks less of the VAR's dynamics. Nor need the two
adjustments be alternatives; raising $p$ and lowering $h$ together, so that neither lever does
all the work on its own, converges naturally on $p=h$, which is exactly the choice
\citet{Antolin-Diaz_Surico2025} make.
\\

None of this is an argument for going long everywhere. \citet{Long_Lag2025} are explicit that
their results speak to structural inference, not to forecasting: when the objective is
predicting unseen data, parsimony still earns its keep, because overfitting rather than
misspecification is the main risk there. It is only when the objective is structural, be it
impulse responses, causal inference, or IRF matching against a DSGE, that the misspecification
concerns described above become the ones that matter.
\\

This is also why the conventional criteria used to choose $p$, Akaike, Hannan-Quinn and
Schwarz, are the wrong tool for that objective. They are built to minimise out-of-sample
forecast error, which rewards parsimony, not to protect against the truncation bias \cite{Humble_VARs_2026,Baumeister2025}. Both \citet{Baumeister2025}
and \citet{Long_Lag2025} argue that a researcher whose aim is structural inference should not
defer to these forecasting criteria, and \citet{GonzalezCasasus_Schorfheide2025} propose an
alternative designed for structural inference.
\\

There is no need, however, to wait for that or other criterias to become standard practice.
\citet{Long_Lag2025} already offer a simpler heuristic that any researcher can apply today:
\textit{"plot the structural moments central to the research question against lag length. If
the structural conclusion dramatically change as the lag length increases, that is clearly
indicating that truncation is important to the question at hand"} \citep[p.~12]{Long_Lag2025}.

\subsection{Model size}
The bias-variance trade-off documented by \cite{Long_Lag2025} applies to all untruncated reduced form VARs.
It does not depend on the number variables in the VAR, nor in the identifying assumption or whether the researcher 
is using a Bayesian or Frequentist method.

The Frequentist researcher might bump into a degrees of freedom problem.
An $n$-variable VAR($p$) has $n + n^2p$ coefficients, and $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ elements in the variance 
covariance matrix. As the lag length grows, the number of parameters to estimate will rapidly increase, and the higher is 
the number of variables in the VAR, the faster will happen.
At some point the number of parameters to estimate will be greater than the number of available data points.
It is shown by \cite{Long_Lag2025} that the degrees of freedom limit is far from being reached by 
small scale VAR with one year of lags. In this work replicating \cite{ACEL2005WP} we stand in the Frequentist side,
so I won't talk regularization techniques or how easy it is to implement long-lag VARs in Bayesian as
they inherently avoid over-parametrisation.

Given the dimension of VAR in analysis, I am going to show that even being close to the limit, 
it is possible to estimate long(er)-lag VARs in the Frequentist approach.

\section{Empirical Results}
\label{sec:results}
In this section I present results from enriched dynamic structure of \cite{ACEL2005WP} VAR
used to estimate their DSGE parameters.
Doing this exercise, I extend the work of \cite{Long_Lag2025} in three different aspects.

First, in Monte Carlo laboratory that \cite{Long_Lag2025} have created to evaluate the three DSGE that have chosen 
to use to generate data, all respect an important assumption, \cite{FVRRSS2007} Poor Man's condition.
All the DSGE admits a VAR($\infty$) representation, and because of this they can evaluate the performance 
of truncated VARs, which are fully informative. They are bivariate, with two variables and two shocks.
The VAR used in \cite{ACEL2011} confronts three shocks with ten observable variables.
The system is therefore stochastically singular, there is no exact VAR($\infty$) representation of the DSGE that 
the VAR is being used to estimate for. But this is not a caveat, is a feature, and is one of the reasons why 
the ACEL used to estimate their model a partial information approach as IRF minimum distance estimator.
\\

The second departure as previously anticipated in the Section \ref{sec:long-lag_VARs} relates on the dimension of 
the VAR employed in this work.
Because the three DGP processes in \cite{Long_Lag2025} are two-variable systems, their VAR remains cheaply 
parametrised even at long lag lengths, and increasing $p$ costs relatively little in terms of degrees of freedom.
The ACEL VAR does not enjoy that luxury. With $K = 10$ variables, a constant and $p$ lags, each equation carries 
$10p + 1$ coefficients, and the sample runs from 1959:Q2 to 2001:Q4, that is $171$ quarterly observations 
before lags are consumed. I will show that concerns related to CI explosions might not occur even in limiting cases.
\\

Third, which is strictly connected to the second, I am using as baseline result the exercises where I am not 
restricting the sample size to the one used in the longest-lag VAR.
The reason is why the results does not vary much, and when they do they do by a small amount and consistently 
with the baseline when the common available data is used. It is shown in Appendix \ref{app:VAR_aligned}.
The other reason is that, in practice, if a researcher has to decide to use a longer-lag VAR rather than a short one, 
the short VAR would be still estimated with as many data points available. 
In particular this argument is even more valid when the VAR has a lot of variables or if the available data 
points is little.

Figure \ref{fig:var_et_irfs_lag12} shows the same instability in a different form. The baseline
responses to an embodied technology shock are front loaded: output, average hours, capacity
utilisation and investment all peak within the first four quarters and then decay. The VAR($12$)

the tenth quarter, reaching their maximum at the end of the horizon. The profile is not delayed, it
is reversed: where the baseline sees the effect of the shock exhausting itself, the long lag VAR
sees it beginning.

What matters for the argument is what happens on impact. Under the recursive scheme most impact
responses to a monetary policy shock are zero by construction, and those that are not differ across
lag orders roughly in proportion to the estimated size of the shock. Nothing of the sort holds here.
The impact response of the relative price of investment to an embodied technology shock, the
variable on which the restriction is anchored, falls from about $-0.45$ in the baseline to about
$-0.1$ at $p = 12$. Inflation responds positively on impact at $p = 4$ and negatively at $p = 8$.
Investment responds positively on impact to a neutral technology shock at $p = 4$ and negatively at
both longer lag orders. These are disagreements at horizon zero, before any propagation has taken
place.

This is the trace that Section \ref{sec:identification_bias} attributes to identification bias
rather than to pure truncation. Pure truncation distorts the propagation of a correctly isolated
shock, so its mark has to accumulate with the horizon and cannot by itself generate a disagreement
at $h = 0$. A restriction imposed on $B(1)$ computed over $p$ lags, when the true polynomial runs to
$p^{*} > p$, isolates a different linear combination of the reduced form innovations, and a
different shock has a different impact effect. The monetary policy responses, identified without the
lag polynomial, keep their sign and their shape and move only in timing. The technology responses,
identified through it, do neither. This is the asymmetry \citet{Ravenna2007} predicts, and it
appears here within the same VAR, on the same sample, with only the identification scheme differing.

Two qualifications are in order. The two channels are not mutually exclusive and the technology
responses are subject to both, so what the figures establish is that the long run identified
responses are the fragile ones, not that pure truncation is absent from them. And longer lags do not
by themselves certify the responses at $p = 12$ as the correct ones. The claim is the weaker one:
what a technology shock does in this VAR is not robust to the lag order, and it is fragile in
exactly the place where the theory says it should be, which is also the place
\citet{ChariKehoeMcGrattan2008} target in their critique of long run identified SVARs.

\subsection{Uncertainty around longer lags estimates}
\label{sec:results_uncertainty}
Conventional view thinks that increasing lag length increase parametrisation, therefore reduce the degrees 
of freedom making uncertainty around the estimates larger. As well explained by \cite{Long_Lag2025}, 
this is for sure part of the story.
But the long-lag VARs authors demonstrate how misspecification lowers the variance of the residuals, and that 
can offset the imprecision caused by additional parameters.
As previously mentioned, in a bivariate system there is plenty of room to increase lag length without 
having big concerns relative to overparametrisation. In \cite{ACEL2011} VAR the concerns are more than realistic, 
the parametrisation grows by a factor of three already large relative to the sample.
If confidence intervals were going to explode anywhere, they would do it here.
\\

They do not.
Table \ref{tab:df_budget} summarise the coefficients and degrees of freedom of the exercise.
The baseline exercise at $p = 4$, the VAR has $410$ coefficients, $167$ available data points and $41$ 
coefficient per regression.
At $p = 12$ the system carries 1210 coefficients, each equation is estimated on $159$ observations with 
$121$ regressors, and only 38 degrees of freedom survive.
The degrees of freedom limit, at which the number of regressors per equation $k$ is greater than the number 
of usable observations $T_u$, pointed out by \cite{Long_Lag2025} in this exercise is at $p = 16$.

Tripling the lag length relative to the ACEL baseline is therefore not a conservative perturbation, 
but it is the largest perturbation the data permit.
This makes the exercise a demanding test for the central claim in \cite{Long_Lag2025}.

whose bands widen at some horizons.
Therefore suggesting that the variance reduction mechanism 
of \cite{Long_Lag2025} operates even at the parametrisation frontier, at least in this work.

\subsubsection*{Is this just overfitting?}

The first objection would be that this is simply overfitting: a VAR with 121 regressors
per equation fits the sample closely, the estimated residuals shrink, and confidence bands
computed from those residuals shrink with them. The objection needs to be taken seriously,
because in sample it cannot be dismissed; reduced misspecification and overfitting have
the same in sample signature, namely a smaller residual variance.
\\

What the objection does not deliver, however, is the conclusion it appears to deliver.
Overfitting pushes the width of the bands in two opposite directions at the same time.
It lowers the variance of the residuals, which narrows the bands, but it also makes the 
coefficient estimates noisier, and noisier coefficients widen them.
Which of the two channels reaches the reported intervals depends on how those intervals are built.
Here the bands are bootstrapped by re-estimating the entire VAR on each artificial sample, so all 
1210 coefficients are estimated again, draw after draw, and the uncertainty they carry is propagated 
into the interval rather than being conditioned away.
If the estimator were unstable at $p = 12$, the re-estimated responses would vary wildly across draws 
and the bands would widen accordingly.
The overfitting story is therefore not complete as it stands. To explain what Figure \ref{fig:var_irfs_all_ci} 
shows, it has to argue that the variance channel beats the parameter uncertainty channel, which is formally 
the same claim \cite{Long_Lag2025} make, only read with the opposite sign.
The burden is symmetric, and it cannot be discharged by pointing at the number of parameters alone.
\\

There is a second, more direct way of looking at the same question.
If the extra lags were merely fitting noise, the first thing to disappear into the fitted part of the model 
would be the large and distinctive movements in the data, because those are exactly the movements a flexible 
model can chase.
Figure \ref{fig:ffr_residuals} plots the in sample residuals of the Federal funds rate equation at $p = 4$ 
and at $p = 12$.

The residuals of the longer VAR are visibly smaller across the whole sample, which is the variance reduction 
discussed above seen from the time series side rather than from the confidence bands.
What survives the reduction is more interesting than its size.
The single largest residual of the sample is the last quarter of 1980, in both specifications, and the years 
of the Volcker disinflation remain the most turbulent stretch of the series at $p = 12$ just as they are at $p = 4$.
The amplitude is much reduced, but the episode is still there and it is still the most prominent feature of the series.
This is not what a model that had absorbed the data into its own parametrisation would look like.
A VAR with 121 regressors per equation that were chasing noise would have had no difficulty in explaining away 
the most conspicuous monetary policy episode of the post war sample, and it would have done so first.
The interpretation that fits the picture better is the one \cite{Long_Lag2025} propose, that the additional lags 
are absorbing serial correlation that the short specification leaves in the residuals, while the genuine 
disturbances remain where the historical record puts them.

\subsubsection*{What the information criteria say}

\citet{ACEL2005WP} report that the AIC, HQ and SW criteria supported $q = 2$, $1$ and $1$ 
lags respectively, yet the authors set $q = 4$. Repeating the same three criteria at $p = 4, 8, 12$ in this 
exercise gives $p = 12$ for AIC and $p = 4$ for both HQ and SW. As already argued in 
Section \ref{sec:truncation}, these criteria are built to minimise out-of-sample forecast error, not to 
guard against truncation bias, so neither \cite{ACEL2005WP} own departure from their criteria nor the disagreement 
found here settles which lag order is appropriate for structural inference; it only confirms that 
forecasting criteria are the not rigth tool for this structural question.


\section{Structural Implications}
\label{sec:structural}

The structural estimates in this section show that the resolution of the micro--macro price
conflict proposed by \cite{ACEL2011} is not robust to the lag structure of the VAR it is
estimated from. The mechanism fails through the very parameter it rests on: as the lag length
increases the cost of varying capital utilisation collapses towards zero, firm specificity stops
being an economically binding constraint, and the two versions of the model end up saying the
same thing about price stickiness.

\subsection{DSGE structural parameters}
\label{sec:results_params}
In Table \ref{tab:dsge_params_comparison_4_8_12} I replicate the results of
\cite{ACEL2005WP} Table 2\footnote{I report \cite{ACEL2005WP} Table 2 in Appendix
\ref{app:acelwp05_table_2}.} across different lag dynamics. The baseline column reproduces
their estimates. The price markup $\lambda_f$ is calibrated, so it carries no standard error.

\FloatBarrier

Two parameters carry the result, and I take them first. The Phillips curve slope $\gamma$ is
unchanged at $0.0402$ between $p = 4$ and $p = 8$, and falls to $0.0129$ at $p = 12$. What
happens to its precision matters as much as the fall itself. The standard error goes from
$0.0246$ at the baseline, where the slope cannot be distinguished from zero, to $0.0042$ at
$p = 12$, where it is significant at the one percent level. The long-lag VAR therefore delivers
an estimate of the Phillips curve slope roughly six times more precise than the baseline one,
which is the variance reduction mechanism of \cite{Long_Lag2025} appearing inside the structural
estimates rather than in the reduced form.
\\

The capacity utilisation cost $\sigma_a$ moves from $1.9938$ at $p = 4$ to $0.1175$ at $p = 8$
and to $0.0042$ at $p = 12$. This is the movement that drives everything that follows. As
discussed in Section \ref{sec:2.2homog_bs_firm-spec_capital}, a $\sigma_a$ close to zero means
that utilisation is almost free to vary, so a firm tied to its own capital can still draw
whatever quantity of capital services it needs at negligible cost, and firm specificity stops
being an economically binding constraint.
\\

There is an important qualification to make about $\sigma_a$, and it strengthens rather than
weakens the argument. At no lag order is $\sigma_a$ statistically distinguishable from zero.
Strictly, therefore, one cannot reject that $\sigma_a$ was zero in ACEL's own specification. The
resolution of the micro--macro conflict rests entirely on the point estimate of a parameter that
the data do not pin down, and what the long-lag VAR does is to move that point estimate into
precisely the region where \cite{ACEL2011} themselves concede that the mechanism fails. The
difference between the baseline and $p = 12$ is not that the parameter becomes insignificant,
since it never was significant, but that at $p = 12$ it is tightly bounded around zero, with a
standard error of $0.0230$, whereas at the baseline the same parameter was consistent with
almost any value in a wide interval.
\\

The remaining parameters move less, but one of them connects the structural estimates to the
impulse responses of Section \ref{sec:results_VARs_IRFs}. Wage stickiness $\xi_w$ falls from
$0.7221$ at the baseline to $0.4838$ at $p = 12$, losing roughly a third of its baseline value,
so labour market frictions become less stringent exactly where the VAR($12$) delivers the hump
shaped real wage response that is absent in the shorter lag versions, Figure
\ref{fig:var_irfs_lag12}. Habit formation $b$ is stable at around $0.70$ for $p = 4$ and
$p = 8$ and rises to $0.86$ at $p = 12$. The money demand elasticity $\epsilon$ fluctuates
mildly and stays close to its baseline value of $0.81$. The investment adjustment cost $S''$
roughly doubles, from $3.2807$ to $6.8431$, and becomes precisely estimated only at $p = 12$,
which translates into a higher cost of adjusting the capital stock over time. It enters the
non-linear expression for $\chi$ alongside $\sigma_a$, but as discussed in Section
\ref{sec:2.2homog_bs_firm-spec_capital} it is not the key driver of the real rigidity.

\subsection{The real rigidity parameter}
\label{sec:results_chi}

Both $\chi$ and the Calvo price stickiness parameters $\xi_p$ are implied parameters. They are
not estimated directly, but recovered by inverting the Phillips curve slope $\gamma$ equation
\eqref{eq:gamma} at the estimated parameters, so the IRF matching estimation does not attach
standard errors to them. To attach a measure of uncertainty I run a Monte Carlo simulation with
one million draws from the joint distribution of the estimated structural parameters. Joint
normality is the assumption already used in the estimation itself, since \cite{ACEL2005WP}
recover the sampling variance of the structural parameters through the delta method, which
assumes exactly that. Appendix \ref{app:delta_method} describes the delta method and Appendix
\ref{app:montecalvo} reports the simulated densities. The intervals reported here
and in the next section are percentiles of the simulated distribution.

\IfFileExists{../../Output/DSGE_Parameters_Comparison/chi_parameter_table_A4_4_8_12.tex}
    {\input{../../Output/DSGE_Parameters_Comparison/chi_parameter_table_A4_4_8_12.tex}}
    {\noindent\fbox{Image Path Broken or File Missing}}
\FloatBarrier

Table \ref{tab:chi_comparison_4_8_12} reports how the real rigidity parameter $\chi$ behaves
across lag orders in the firm-specific capital model, against the homogeneous model where it is
set to one by construction. At the baseline $\chi_{fs} = 0.0307$, with a $95$ percent simulated
interval of $[0.004, 0.146]$. The attenuation is close to total: a firm that is able to reset
its price moves it by about three percent of what it would move it in the absence of the real
rigidity. At $p = 8$ the parameter rises to $0.2030$, and at $p = 12$ to $0.8671$, with a $95$
percent interval of $[0.342, 0.959]$. The baseline and the long-lag intervals do not overlap, so
the movement is not an artefact of the imprecision of either estimate.
\\

An attenuation factor of $0.03$ means that firm specificity does almost all of the work
in dampening price adjustment, while a factor of $0.87$ means that it does almost none of it and
the firm-specific model behaves nearly as the homogeneous one does. The question that matters
for \cite{ACEL2011} is whether $\chi$ is small enough to rationalise a low $\xi_p$, and at $p = 12$ it is not.

\subsection{Calvo price stickiness parameters and the collapse of the firm-specific mechanism}
\label{sec:results_calvo}

Having in mind equation \eqref{eq:gamma}, the results on the Calvo price stickiness parameters
reported in Table \ref{tab:calvo_price_stickiness_4_8_12} follow directly from the two movements
just described.

\IfFileExists{../../Output/DSGE_Parameters_Comparison/calvo_price_stickiness_table_A4_4_8_12_lags.tex}

In the homogeneous capital model $\chi = 1$, so the whole burden of delivering a small $\gamma$
falls on price stickiness alone. The parameter is $0.8213$ at $p = 4$ and at $p = 8$, with a
$95$ percent simulated interval of $[0.747, 0.936]$ at the baseline, and it rises to $0.8958$ at
$p = 12$, with an interval of $[0.869, 0.923]$. The flatter Phillips curve of the long-lag VAR
therefore makes the homogeneous model worse on its own terms. The friction it requires in order
to match the aggregate evidence becomes stronger, not weaker, and it was already implausibly
strong at the baseline.
\\

In the firm-specific model the parameter moves a great deal more. It is $0.3372$ at the
baseline, with a $95$ percent interval of $[0.057, 0.709]$, rises to $0.6448$ at $p = 8$, with
an interval of $[0.413, 0.853]$, and reaches $0.8884$ at $p = 12$, with an interval of
$[0.815, 0.898]$. The baseline point estimate lies outside the $95$ percent interval of the
$p = 8$ distribution, where only $1.1$ percent of the draws fall at or below it. At $p = 12$ the
separation is complete, since no draw at all falls at or below the baseline value. Whatever the
firm-specific model says about price stickiness at $p = 4$, it is not what the same model says
once the VAR is given a richer lag structure.

\subsection{Implied duration of prices}
\label{sec:results_duration}

Under Calvo price setting the implied duration, average time between re-optimisations, is
$D = \frac{1}{1-\xi_p}$, and it is here that the size of the gap between the two models becomes
meaningful.

\IfFileExists{../../Output/DSGE_Parameters_Comparison/implied_duration_A4_4_8_12.tex}
    {\input{../../Output/DSGE_Parameters_Comparison/implied_duration_A4_4_8_12.tex}}

\FloatBarrier

Table \ref{tab:implied_duration_4_8_12} shows how increasing the lag length of the VAR used to
estimate the \cite{ACEL2011} DSGE brings back the micro--macro price duration conflict that
firm-specific capital was introduced to resolve. At the baseline the firm-specific model implies
that prices are re-optimised on average every $1.5$ quarters, against $5.6$ quarters in the
homogeneous model, and it is this difference that allows ACEL to claim that only the
firm-specific version is compatible with the micro evidence. At $p = 8$ the firm-specific
duration almost doubles, to $2.8$ quarters, which is still half the homogeneous figure but
already a long way from the micro evidence. At $p = 12$ the two models imply $9.0$ and $9.6$
quarters.
\\

The homogeneous capital model, whose implied duration rises from $5.6$ to $9.6$ quarters, 
was already in open conflict with the micro evidence at the baseline, and now
ends up requiring prices to remain fixed for well over two years. The long-lag results therefore
do not point at the homogeneous capital model at the expense of the firm-specific one. They
push both models into the same implausible region, and the conclusion is not that one version
wins the argument, but that the reconciliation \cite{ACEL2011} offer is not available at all
once the VAR is allowed a richer lag structure.
\\

This outcome is the one their own argument anticipates. The reconciliation works only if varying
capital utilisation is costly enough to make firm specificity bind, and the models' authors state
that ``to the extent that capital utilization rates can easily be varied, the assumption of
firm-specific capital loses its ability to rationalize low values of $\xi_p$''
\citep[p.~227]{ACEL2011}. The long-lag VAR drives $\sigma_a$ to essentially zero, which is
exactly the case they describe. I do not claim that $p = 12$ delivers the true structural
parameters, and nothing here establishes that the firm-specific mechanism is wrong in itself.
What the exercise establishes is that the conclusion drawn from it is not robust to a choice,
the lag order of the VAR, that was not treated as a substantive modelling decision.

\section{Delta Method}
\label{app:delta_method}
Following the standard error estimation strategy for the DSGE structural parameters chosen by \cite{ACEL2005WP}
and well explained in their footnote number 14.
\\

Given the IRF minimum distance estimator Equation \ref{eq:irfme}, 
let the criterion be denoted as $L(\zeta,\hat{\Psi}) \equiv (\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\zeta))' V^{-1} (\hat{\Psi} - \Psi(\zeta))$ 
so that $L_1(\hat{\zeta},\hat{\Psi}) = 0$.
\\

Note that the mapping in \ref{eq:irfme} is given by $\hat{\zeta} = f(\hat{\Psi})$. In order to obtain 
the sampling variance of the estimator, $\hat{\zeta}$, as a function of the sampling variance of $\hat{\Psi}$, 
the delta function method approximates $f(\hat{\Psi})$ by its linear expansion about the true value of $\Psi$, $\Psi^0$.
The Taylor's expansion is given by, $f(\hat{\Psi}) \approx f(\Psi^0) + f'(\Psi^0)(\hat{\Psi} - \Psi^0)$.
Here $f(\Psi^0) = \zeta^0$, where $\zeta^0$ is the true unknown value of $\zeta$, by the consistency of the estimator.
\\

Then $\sqrt{T}(\hat{\zeta_T}-\zeta^0)$ is asymptotically Normally distributed with zero mean and variance equal to 
$f'(\Psi^0)Wf'(\Psi^0)^{T}$, where the superscript T indicates the transposition operator, 
and $W$ is the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix of $\sqrt{T}(\hat{\Psi}-\Psi)$.
\\

Then, implicit function theorem is used to approximate $f'(\Psi^0)$ by $-L_{11}(\zeta^0,\Psi^0)^{-1}L_{12}(\zeta^0,\Psi^0)$.
Then, $V$ is not selected as random, in practice a diagonal matrix is built using the VAR IRFs variances $W$.
In addition, $W$ is replaced by its sample estimate, as are $\zeta^0$ and $\Psi^0$ in the expression for $f'$.

\section{Monte Carlo Inference on the Calvo Parameters}
\label{app:montecalvo}

Neither $\chi$ nor the Calvo price stickiness parameters $\xi_p$ are estimated directly. They
are recovered by inverting the non-linear mapping in equation \eqref{eq:gamma} at the estimated
structural parameters, so the IRF matching estimator attaches no standard error to either of
them. The delta method of Appendix \ref{app:delta_method} gives the joint sampling variance-covariance
$V_{DSGE}$ of the estimated structural parameters, but propagating that uncertainty through a
non-linear mapping analytically is not tractable here, so I do it by simulation.
\\

I draw the full vector of structural parameters jointly from a multivariate normal distribution
centred at the point estimates $\hat{\zeta}$, with variance-covariance $V_{DSGE}$, for $N = 1{,}000{,}000$
draws. Drawing the parameters jointly, rather than varying $\gamma$ alone, matters because
$\chi$ and $\xi_p$ depend on several parameters at once, and those parameters are themselves
correlated: the simulation has to preserve the correlation structure encoded in $V_{DSGE}$, not
just the marginal uncertainty of each parameter taken on its own.
\\

Five of the estimated parameters enter the mapping directly: the Phillips curve slope $\gamma$,
the capacity utilisation cost $\sigma_a$, the investment adjustment cost $S''$, habit formation
$b$, and the money demand elasticity $\epsilon$. The remaining calibrated parameters are held
fixed at their original values. For every draw of these five parameters I recover the implied
Calvo parameters using the ''findksip'' routine from the \citet{ACEL2005WP} replication
code. In the homogeneous capital model $\chi \equiv 1$ by construction, so equation
\eqref{eq:gamma} collapses to a quadratic in $\xi_{p,hom}$ and the routine solves it in closed
form. In the firm-specific model $\chi$ is itself a non-linear function of the draw, so the
routine searches a grid of candidate values of $\xi_{p,fs}$, brackets the point at which the
implied $\gamma$ crosses the target value, and refines the root numerically.
\\

Not every draw returns an economically meaningful solution. Some draws imply a $\gamma$ for
which no admissible root exists, or a complex valued one, and these are discarded rather than
recorded as zero or as a boundary value. The share of draws that return a valid solution is
$78.6\%$ at $p=4$, $31.8\%$ at $p=8$, and $49.2\%$ at $p=12$. The empirical distributions of
$\xi_{p,fs}$ and $\xi_{p,hom}$ across the valid draws, plotted below, are what the percentile
intervals reported in Sections \ref{sec:results_chi} and \ref{sec:results_calvo} are computed
from.

Here I plot the probability density functions (PDF) and the cumulative density functions (CDF) derived 
from the Monte Carlo simulation.
