    \begin{itemize}
        \item VARs can be used to estimate DSGE frameworks by IRFs minimization matching
    \end{itemize}
    
    \begin{itemize}  
        \item If the VAR is mispecified the DSGE might also be so
    \end{itemize}
    \vspace{0.1cm}
    
    \textbf{\textcolor{PadovaRed}{Why it is relevant?}}
    \pause
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{"The quality of our strategy (IRFs matching) depends on the \textbf{ability of identified VARs to generate reliable estimates} of the dynamic response of economic variables to shocks."}
        \vspace{0.1cm}
        \hfill {\small \textbf{Altig et al. (2011) p. 3, fn. 10} }
        \pause

        \item \textit{``If the VAR adequately caputeres the DGP, IRFs are optimal at all horizons. 
        \\
        If the \textbf{VAR is mispecified}, however, then the \textbf{specification errors will be computed at each horizon}''}
        \vspace{0.1cm}
        \hfill {\small \textbf{\citep{Ramey2016}, p. 83} }
        \pause
        
        \item VARs and DSGEs are instruments used by central banks to make policy decisions
        \item Misspecification can deliver distorted results mis informing the central banker
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

% ILLUSTRATION OF THE PROBLEM: TRUNCATION BIAS 
% =======================================

\begin{frame}{Illustration of the problem: Truncation Bias}
    \begin{equation}
        Y_t = B_1Y_{t-1} + \cdots + B_pY_{t-p} + u_t, \qquad \mathbb{E}(u_t u_t') = \Sigma
    \end{equation}

    \begin{equation}
        B(L) = B_1L + \cdots + B_pL^p
    \end{equation}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item The VAR with $p < p^{*}$ suffers from truncation bias.\footnote{Where $p^{*}$ denotes the DGP true lag length.}

        \item Lag truncation biases the reduced form coefficients $B(L)$ and the reduced form variance covariance matrix $\Sigma$, which are the two main ingredients for IRF estimation.
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{IRF} = f\bigl(B(L),\Sigma\bigr)
    \end{equation}
\end{frame}

    \begin{itemize}

        \item This variance reduction counteracts the imprecision caused by increased parametrisation
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% =======================================
% ACEL'S MECHANISM 1
% =======================================

\begin{frame}{ACEL's DSGE Mechanism}
    Classic NK models account for inflation inertia by assuming that firms re-optimise their prices every six quarters or even less often
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In \citep{ACEL2005WP} inflation is inertial despite firms re-optimising prices on average every 1.5 quarters
        \item Inertia is explained by the fact that, when firms re-optimise, they do it by a small amount because each firm's short run marginal cost is increasing in its own output
        \item The key assumption is that, in any given period, a firm's capital stock is pre-determined and firm-specific
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    They build two models on \citep{CEE2005}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Homogeneous capital
        \item Firm-specific capital
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% =======================================
% ACEL'S MECHANISM 2
% =======================================

\begin{frame}{ACEL's DSGE Mechanism (Cont'd)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item When log-linearized, the only difference in the characterizing equilibrium equations lies in the Phillips Curve
\end{itemize}

\begin{align}
    \Delta\hat{\pi}_t &= \mathbb{E} [\beta\Delta\hat{\pi}_{t+1} + \gamma\hat{s}_t|\Omega_t]
    \label{eq:Phillips_Curve}
    \\
    \gamma &= \frac{(1-\xi_p)(1-\beta\xi_p)}{\xi_p}\chi
    \label{eq:Phillips_Curve_Slope}
\end{align}

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\hat{s}_t$ is the economy-wide marginal cost, $\gamma$ the Phillips Curve slope, $\xi_p$ the Calvo price stickiness parameter
    \item $\chi$ reduces the sensitivity of inflation to marginal costs in the firm-specific capital model
    \item The two models are observationally equivalent in terms of prices and quantities: macro data identify $\gamma$, so the difference between the two models lies in the implied $\xi_p$
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% =======================================
% ACEL'S MECHANISM 3
% =======================================

\begin{frame}{ACEL's DSGE Mechanism (Cont'd)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the \textbf{Homogeneous} model, $\chi = 1$: $\gamma$ depends only on $\beta$ and $\xi_p$
    \item In the \textbf{Firm-specific} model, $\chi < 1$ and captures two forces that make a firm move its price \textit{less} when it can reset:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the costlier it is to vary capital utilization, the steeper the firm's own marginal cost curve
        \item the more elastic the firm's demand, the larger the fall in output (and hence in marginal cost) after a price increase
    \end{itemize}
    \vspace{0.3cm}
    \item So $\chi$ governs \textbf{how much} a firm moves its price
    \item $\xi_p$ \textbf{how often}, with implied duration $1/(1-\xi_p)$
\end{itemize}
 Since $\gamma$ is estimated by aggregate data, a smaller $\chi$ imply a smaller $\xi_p$
\end{frame}

% =======================================
% ACEL'S RESULTS
% =======================================

\begin{frame}{ACEL(2005) Results}
    The estimated $\gamma$ is 0.040, which implies:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Homogeneous} capital: $\xi_p = 0.82$, firms change prices every 5.6 quarters
        \item \textbf{Firm-specific} capital: firms change prices every 1.5 quarters
    \end{itemize}
    The gap arises because firms' demand curves are highly elastic and their marginal cost curves are very steep: the same $\gamma$ is delivered by frequent but small price adjustments.
\\
\vspace{0.1cm}
\textbf{Only the firm-specific capital model reconciles the micro-macro pricing conflict}
\vspace{0.5cm}
